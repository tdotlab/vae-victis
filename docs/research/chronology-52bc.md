# Chronology of 52 BC, and Uxellodunum (51 BC)

Research track 1 of 4. Scope: when things happened in Vercingetorix's revolt, how far apart they were, and whether the game's historical clock (DESIGN.md, "The historical clock") holds up. Also covers the Cadurci and Lucterius in 52-51 BC, and the siege of Uxellodunum.

Confidence labels: **attested** means an ancient text says it. **Inferred** means a modern reconstruction. **Uncertain** means scholars disagree, and the entry names who.

"Roman" dates are the Roman civil calendar of the time. "Jul." dates are the proleptic Julian calendar, which tracks the seasons. In 52 BC the two were about three to six weeks apart (see section 2.1).

---

## 1. Summary

1. **Caesar gives no calendar dates for 52 BC.** BG VII has only seasons, day counts and distances. Every date in this file is a reconstruction anchored on Clodius's murder (18 Jan Roman, Asconius) and on seasonal remarks in Caesar. The best current reconstruction is Raaflaub & Ramsey (Histos 11, 2017), used as the backbone here.
2. **The game's clock is too fast after about day 50.** It is roughly right for the winter phase, but the year ran much longer than 240 days. Counting from the Cenabum massacre (about 9 Jan 52 Jul.): Avaricum falls about **day 137** (game: 85), the Gergovia defeat about **day 190** (game: 120), and Alesia surrenders about **day 284**, mid to late October Julian (game: 240, "late September"). Brunaux's shorter scheme puts the surrender about day 262. Nobody credible fits the whole year into 240 days.
3. **The Cevennes crossing came about day 56, not day 25.** Caesar first had to learn of the revolt in Italy (about day 21), travel to Narbo (about day 44), secure the Province, and gather troops among the Helvii. R&R put the crossing about 5 Mar Jul.
4. **Gergovia was never "taken" by Vercingetorix in the military sense.** He was expelled from it by his uncle Gobannitio, raised the rural poor, and came back to drive out his opponents (VII.4). It happened within days to a couple of weeks of Cenabum. Game day 5 is plausible.
5. **DESIGN.md's specific claims mostly check out against Caesar:** more than twenty Biturigan towns burned in one day (VII.15), barely 800 of about 40,000 escaping Avaricum (VII.28), the Aeduan seizure of Noviodunum with the hostages sent to Bibracte (VII.55), one captive per soldier at Alesia (VII.89), and the spring and the cut hands at Uxellodunum (VIII.41-44). Three need fixing:
   - "Mandubii starve between the lines" comes from Cassius Dio (XL.40), not Caesar. Caesar only says he refused to take them in.
   - One captive per soldier excluded the Aedui and Arverni. About 20,000 of those were sent home (VII.89-90).
   - At Uxellodunum the hands were cut from "all who had borne arms", and their lives were spared (VIII.44).
6. **The Cadurci matter in 52.** They are in Vercingetorix's first wave of allies (VII.4). Lucterius, a Cadurcan, led the southern thrust against the Ruteni, Nitiobriges and Gabali and toward Narbo (VII.5, 7-8). The Cadurci were later sent with the Ruteni to raid the Volcae Arecomici (VII.64). For the Alesia relief they were counted inside the Arverni's 35,000, not given a quota of their own (VII.75). This gives the player's home region a real place in the war from the first month.
7. **Uxellodunum fell in late summer 51.** R&R put the capitulation at the end of July Jul. (end of August Roman). It is very probably the Puy d'Issolud near Vayrac (Lot), where excavations at the Fontaine de Loulié found Roman tunnels and hundreds of iron arrow and bolt heads. The town was in Lucterius's clientela (VIII.32), which makes him a plausible patron of a Cadurcan hill fort like Brigodunon.
8. **News and armies moved at very different speeds.** Shouted relays covered about 160 Roman miles (about 237 km) between sunrise and the end of the first watch (VII.3). Caesar's couriers made about 80 km a day. Caesar himself rode about 75 km a day on forced marches. Legions on campaign managed 20-25 km a day, much less when besieging or foraging. The game's fast "rumour" and slow armies are right in kind.

## 2. Findings by topic

### 2.1 The calendar problem

**The Roman calendar in 52 BC was out of step with the seasons.** This is attested in outline and inferred in detail. 52 BC (AUC 702) was intercalated: a month of 27 days was inserted after 23/24 February. The year was therefore 378 days long. Asconius dates Pompey's appointment as sole consul to the 24th of the intercalary month (Asconius, *Pro Milone* argument, 36 Clark), which proves the intercalation.

Converting Roman dates to Julian ones depends on which reconstruction of the pre-Julian calendar you follow. The two tables I checked agree to within about two days:

| Roman date (52 BC) | Marinone (after Holzapfel/Groebe) | Bennett |
|---|---|---|
| Kal. Ian. 52 | 21 Nov 53 Jul. | 19 Nov 53 |
| Clodius killed, 18 Jan | 8 Dec 53 | 6 Dec 53 |
| Kal. Mart. | 9 Feb | 7 Feb |
| Kal. Oct. | 6 Sep | 4 Sep |
| Kal. Ian. 51 | 4 Dec 52 | 2 Dec 52 |

So before the intercalary month the Roman date ran about 41 days ahead of the season. After it, the lead was about 20-28 days. In practice, a Roman "February" in 52 was early-to-mid January in season, and a Roman "November" was October.

Other conversions exist and give different results. French scholarship following Bloch and Carcopino (1935) puts Clodius's murder on 30 Dec 53 Jul. and Cenabum on 13 Feb Roman = 23 Jan Jul. That is about two weeks later than R&R. Treat any single Julian date for 52 BC as ±2 weeks.

**For the game:** day numbers should follow the seasons, so use Julian dates. The Roman month names a Gaul would never have used anyway.

### 2.2 The anchors

The fixed points come from outside Caesar:

- Clodius killed on 18 Jan Roman (Asconius 31 Clark). **Attested.**
- The senate passes its emergency decree, and Pompey is told to levy troops, before the intercalary month (Asconius 34 Clark). Caesar says he heard of the decree that all Italian iuniores should take the military oath, and held a levy across the Province (VII.1). **Attested.**
- Pompey made sole consul on 24 Intercalaris (Asconius 36 Clark). **Attested.** Caesar's own phrase is that city affairs had been improved "by Pompey's courage" before he left Italy (VII.6). That puts his departure after this date. **Inferred** (R&R).

Caesar's own seasonal remarks:

- The Cevennes are crossed "durissimo tempore anni", through snow six feet deep (VII.8). **Attested.**
- "Reliquam partem hiemis", the rest of the winter, worries him at Agedincum (VII.10). **Attested.**
- At Avaricum there is cold and constant rain throughout the siege (VII.24). **Attested.**
- After Avaricum, winter is "almost over" ("iam prope hieme confecta"), and the season calls him to campaign (VII.32). **Attested.** R&R anchor this to about 1 Jun Jul. and admit it may be several days too early.
- On the Allier, the river is not usually fordable before autumn (VII.35). At the Loire after Gergovia, the river is swollen "ex nivibus", from snowmelt (VII.55). Caesar then finds grain and cattle in the fields (VII.56). **Attested.**
- The next winter's campaign against the Bituriges begins "pridie Kal. Ianuarias" (VIII.2). This is the only calendar date in BG VII-VIII. **Attested.**

Note that Caesar's "winter" runs very late. Avaricum was taken in cold rain in what R&R reckon is late May Julian. This is consistent with the BG's habit of counting the campaigning season from when fodder was available, not from the equinox.

**The day column has real uncertainty in both directions.** Three things drive it:

- The start. If Bloch-Carcopino's Cenabum on 23 Jan Jul. is right, every later day count drops by about 14.
- The end-of-winter anchor (VII.32). R&R say theirs could be a few days early.
- Alesia's duration. Caesar gives no total. R&R reckon the blockade lasted from about late August to 19 October Julian, nearly two months. Brunaux (2012) dates the relief battles to 20-26 Sept and the surrender to 27 Sept (Julian), using the moon phase for the night attack. That is about day 262. Some older accounts compress Alesia to about six weeks.

**Where scholars disagree:**

- *Whether the plot began before Clodius's murder.* Jullian (*Histoire de la Gaule* III, 415 n. 1) thought it predated the news from Rome. Holmes (1911, p. 736) and R&R treat the news as the trigger, as Caesar says (VII.1). **Uncertain.** It doesn't change the clock.
- *Napoleon III's relay posts for the Cenabum news.* Napoleon III and Jullian imagined organised stations of criers. Holmes and Long thought ordinary shouting from field to field, as Caesar describes, was enough. **Uncertain.**
- *Where the legions concentrated.* Caesar says Agedincum (VII.10). Stoffel argued for somewhere further east, possibly around Châtillon. Holmes rejects this. **Uncertain**, minor.
- *Dio's version.* Cassius Dio (XL.33-41) omits Cenabum, says the Arverni killed all the Romans, and has Caesar turn to the Lingones because the Loire stopped him (XL.38). That contradicts Caesar's narrative of the Loire crossing. Dio also says winter had set in by the fall of Avaricum, which contradicts BG VII.32. Dio is two and a half centuries later and less reliable here. **Prefer Caesar.**
- *Plutarch's winter.* Plutarch (*Caesar* 26) has frozen rivers and snow-covered forests. This is rhetorical colour for the Cevennes campaign, not a dating tool.
- *Danielle Porte* (*L'imposture Alésia*, 2004) dates the Alesia siege 18 Aug-7 Oct and the surrender to 7 Oct. Her book argues that Alesia was at Chaux-des-Crotenay, against the excavated site at Alise-Sainte-Reine. That position is fringe; don't use her dates. A widely copied popular website (Livius) gives 30 Aug or 3 Sept Julian for the end, without sources and with internal inconsistencies. Don't use it either.

### 2.4 How fast news and armies moved

- **Shouted relay:** what happened at Cenabum at sunrise was known among the Arverni before the end of the first watch, about 160 Roman miles (about 237 km). Caesar says this is the Gallic custom: important news is shouted across fields and districts and passed on (VII.3). **Attested.** That is roughly 15-17 hours for 237 km, about 15 km/h. DESIGN.md says "by nightfall". Caesar's "end of the first watch" means about three hours after sunset, so close enough; "by midnight" is more exact.
- **Roman couriers:** R&R reckon about 80 km a day for dispatch riders, based on Cicero's letters and the Agedincum-Ravenna message (about 1,550 km in 19 days). **Inferred.**
- **Caesar in a hurry:** Ravenna to Narbo, about 1,030 km in 13 days (about 80 km/day). Vienne to the Lingones, about 245 km in 4 days, riding day and night (VII.9). **Inferred** from attested routes.
- **Legions:** Agedincum to Vellaunodunum in a day, Vellaunodunum to Cenabum in two (VII.11). Gergovia to the Allier in three. Nearly six days from the Allier crossing to Gergovia (VII.35-36). That is 20-25 km a day with baggage. Caesar's night march against Litaviccus covered 25 miles each way with four legions in about a day (VII.40-41). That was exceptional and he says so.
- **Large Gallic forces** gathered slowly. The relief army took about a month to assemble from the moment the cavalry left Alesia (VII.71, 75-77).

**For the game:** a cell is about 12 km, and plain hours per cell are 4. That gives about 3 km/h on foot, about 24-36 km a day. That is right for a fit traveller on foot, faster than a legion with its baggage, slower than a rider. The 2.2 winter multiplier for mountains fits Caesar's Cevennes (VII.8): he says the crossing only worked because the snow was shovelled clear.

### 2.5 Specific claims in DESIGN.md, checked

- **"The Bituriges burn more than twenty towns."** Correct: "uno die amplius XX urbes Biturigum incenduntur" (VII.15). Other states did the same. It was Vercingetorix's policy, voted by the council (VII.14-15). The Bituriges then begged the *Gallic council*, not Caesar, to spare Avaricum, "nearly the most beautiful city in all Gaul". Vercingetorix opposed sparing it at first, then gave way. **Attested.**
- **Avaricum, 800 of about 40,000.** Correct: "ex omni numero, qui fuit circiter milium XL, vix DCCC" reached Vercingetorix (VII.28). Caesar also says the soldiers spared no one, old men, women or children, remembering Cenabum. **Attested.** The figure is Caesar's and may be inflated; no other source checks it.
- **Noviodunum.** The town is Noviodunum *of the Aedui*, on the Loire (VII.55), not the Biturigan Noviodunum Caesar took in VII.12-13. Two different places with the same name. The location is often put at Nevers, but this is not settled; Pouilly-sur-Loire and others have been proposed. **Uncertain.** The Aeduan nobles **Eporedorix and Viridomarus** did it, after hearing that Litaviccus had been welcomed at Bibracte. They killed the guards and the Roman traders, split the money and horses between them, had the hostages taken to the magistrate at Bibracte, burned the town, and carried off or spoiled the grain. Caesar had stored there all the hostages of Gaul, grain, the army's money, much of his own and the army's baggage, and horses bought in Italy and Spain. **Attested** (VII.55).
- **Hostages as a threat.** After this, the Aedui used the hostages to frighten states that hesitated, threatening to put them to death (VII.63: "horum supplicio dubitantes territant"). This is directly relevant to the game's hostage premise. **Attested.**
- **The Bibracte assembly.** Caesar calls it a council of all Gaul at Bibracte. All endorsed Vercingetorix as commander; the Aedui had wanted the command; the Remi, Lingones and Treveri stayed away (VII.63). Eporedorix and Viridomarus submitted unwillingly. **Attested.** It was not a spring meeting; see the timeline.
- **"Mandubii starve between the lines."** Caesar says the Mandubii were expelled with their wives and children, begged to be taken as slaves and fed, and Caesar posted guards and refused them (VII.78). He doesn't say what happened next. Cassius Dio says they died miserably between the town and the Roman works (XL.40). **Attested, but by Dio, not Caesar.** It's a fair inference and widely accepted; just credit it correctly if anything in game says "Caesar wrote".
- **"One captive per soldier."** Correct, with an exception: the Aedui and Arverni were held back, the rest distributed as booty, one per head across the army (VII.89). About 20,000 Aeduan and Arvernian captives were then returned to their peoples to win them back (VII.90). **Attested.**
- **Uxellodunum: the spring, and the hands.** Correct. The tunnels cut and diverted the veins of the spring, and the defenders took the drying of a perennial spring as the gods' will and surrendered (VIII.43). Caesar spared their lives but cut off the hands of all who had borne arms, as a warning (VIII.44). **Attested** (Hirtius).

### 2.6 The Cadurci and Lucterius, 52-51 BC

- **First wave.** The Cadurci are among the peoples Vercingetorix won over quickly in his first weeks (VII.4: Senones, Parisii, Pictones, Cadurci, Turoni, Aulerci, Lemovices, Andes and the Ocean peoples). **Attested.** So by game day 5-15 the Cadurci were formally in the war. That doesn't mean every hill fort agreed; Hirtius says that in 51 some Cadurci helped Lucterius willingly and others could not stop him (VIII.34).
- **Lucterius.** Caesar calls him a Cadurcan "of the utmost daring" (VII.5). Sent with part of the forces to the Ruteni, he won them over, then took hostages from the Nitiobriges and the Gabali and gathered a large force to break into the Province toward Narbo (VII.7). Caesar's arrival at Narbo and his garrisons among the Ruteni of the Province, the Volcae Arecomici and the Tolosates stopped him: "represso iam Lucterio et remoto" (VII.8). **Attested.** Timing: about days 10-45. The game's day 12 for "lucterius" is reasonable as the start.
- **Later in 52.** After Bibracte, Vercingetorix sent the Gabali and the nearest Arvernian cantons against the Helvii, and the Ruteni and Cadurci to ravage the lands of the Volcae Arecomici (VII.64). **Attested.** Date: about August Julian, days 205-230. This is a natural hook for a Cadurcan war band leaving home in the game's Act II.
- **The Alesia relief.** The Arverni's quota was 35,000 *including* the Eleuteti, Cadurci, Gabali and Vellavii, "who are used to being under Arvernian command" (VII.75). **Attested.** The reading is debated. Some editors take "Eleutetis Cadurcis" as one people, the "Cadurci Eleuteri" (independent or free Cadurci), against the manuscripts' separate listing. **Uncertain.** Either way, the Cadurci were Arvernian clients in this period and had no separate quota. Whether Lucterius himself was at Alesia is unclear: Hirtius says he "had tried the risk of that fortune" (VIII.34), which may mean he fought there or only that he knew what a siege meant. **Uncertain.**
- **51 BC.** After Alesia, Lucterius joined Drappes of the Senones, with a band of about 2,000 or fewer, many of them runaway slaves and exiles, again aiming at the Province (VIII.30). With Caninius Rebilus following, they stopped in Cadurcan territory. Lucterius had been powerful among his people before the war and had a reputation as a leader of revolts. He seized Uxellodunum, a town strong by nature, which "had been in his clientela" (VIII.32). **Attested.** Hirtius adds that he took the townspeople with him.
- **The end of Lucterius.** After the siege he kept moving and never stayed long in one place, fearing capture. The Arvernian Epasnactus, a firm friend of Rome, caught him and delivered him to Caesar in chains (VIII.44). **Attested.** Drappes starved himself to death in captivity (VIII.44).

### 2.7 Uxellodunum, 51 BC

**Date.** Hirtius gives no calendar date. R&R: Rebilus arrives about 10 Jul Roman / 8 Jun Jul.; Drappes and Lucterius had arrived early that month. Rebilus blocks the grain convoys in the second half of the month. Fabius, then Caesar, come by late July or early August Roman. The town surrenders at the end of August Roman / end of July Julian. Caesar then goes to Aquitania for "the last part of the summer" (VIII.46). **Inferred.** The siege as a whole ran about two months.

**The siege, from Hirtius.** Rebilus built three camps on high ground and began a line of works (VIII.33). The defenders, fearing a repeat of Alesia, left 2,000 men in the town and went out with the rest to gather grain (VIII.34). Rebilus caught Lucterius's convoy at night about the tenth hour; Lucterius escaped with a few. The next day Drappes's camp, about 12 miles away, was taken and Drappes captured (VIII.35-36). Fabius arrived (VIII.37). Caesar, knowing all Gaul knew he had only one summer left in his command and that a successful holdout would encourage others, came with all his cavalry (VIII.39), arriving when nobody expected him (VIII.40). The town had plenty of grain, so he went for its water. Archers, slingers and artillery cut off access to the river (VIII.40). A large spring under the wall, at a gap of about 300 feet in the river's loop, was the last source. He built a ramp 60 feet high with a ten-storey tower to command it, and dug tunnels toward its source (VIII.41). The defenders rolled burning barrels down onto the works (VIII.42). The tunnels finally cut and diverted the spring's veins; the defenders took it as the gods' will and surrendered (VIII.43). **Attested.**

**The site.** Several candidates have been put forward since the 16th century: Capdenac, Luzech, the Puy d'Issolud near Vayrac, and Murcens, among others. Napoleon III's commission favoured the Puy d'Issolud in the 1860s, and J.-B. Cessac excavated there, finding in 1865 a tunnel at the Fontaine de Loulié. Excavations directed by J.-P. Girault from the 1990s found more tunnel sections in 1998, interpreted as Roman siege galleries, and a large deposit of iron arrowheads and catapult bolt heads (catalogue numbers run to about 270 items). Metallurgical studies by G. Renoux, F. Dabosi and J.-M. Pailler analysed the arrowheads and placed them in the Late Iron Age / Caesarian period. The identification has been officially accepted in France since the early 2000s (commonly given as 2001; I did not find the ministry document itself). **Inferred, with strong consensus**: the arms finds and the fit to Hirtius's spring description are hard to beat. Most of the detailed reports are hosted by the Uxellodunum association site, which is closely tied to the excavators, so the evidence is not independent of the people who made the claim. I didn't find a recent dissenting peer-reviewed argument.

**Geography for the game.** The Puy d'Issolud overlooks the Dordogne (Duranius) valley near Vayrac, and Hirtius's river that "almost surrounds" the hill is usually identified with the Tourmente and Dordogne. The game puts Uxellodunum "on its cliff above the Duranius" at cell [10,28]; that fits.

| Uxellodunum falls (epilogue) | epilogue | about day 570 (end of July 51 Jul.) | 550-590 | VIII.43-46; R&R |

Main corrections:

1. **Move the Cevennes from day 25 to about day 56.** Caesar can't be in the mountains before he has heard the news in Italy, travelled to Narbo and organised the Province (VII.6-8).
2. **Everything from Avaricum on is about 50-70 days later than the game has it.** Avaricum falls about day 137, not 85. Gergovia about 190, not 120. Noviodunum and Bibracte about 196 and 215, not 130 and 140.
3. **The year is about 280 days long, not 240**, unless the game adopts Brunaux's date for Alesia (about 262) or the later Bloch-Carcopino start (about −14 days throughout). Even the shortest credible scheme exceeds 240 by about two weeks.
4. **The gap between Gergovia and Noviodunum is days, not ten days.** Caesar's narrative runs straight on (VII.53-55). The gap between Noviodunum and Bibracte is a few weeks.

**Design option, not a correction:** the game could keep a compressed 240-day clock by declaring it a game convention. That would mean pacing that sometimes contradicts Caesar's day counts (25 days for the Avaricum ramp, "quintis castris" to Gergovia). The design rule ("don't contradict what's known") points the other way. I'd re-time.

### 3.2 Seasons in game_state.gd

The current boundaries are winter to day 45, spring to 135, summer to 230, autumn after. Against the texts:

- Caesar's own "winter" lasts at least to the end of the Avaricum siege (VII.24, VII.32), about day 137-144 by R&R. That is a campaign-season "winter", meaning no fodder, not frost. In Julian terms, that is late May.
- The Cevennes, about day 56, early March Julian, is deep snow.
- The Loire in late July Julian is still "swollen from snowmelt" (VII.55). Odd, but attested; the Loire's Massif Central headwaters can run high late in a wet year.
- Alesia ends in mid-October Julian: autumn.

Suggested boundaries for the corrected clock, Julian by the calendar: winter days 1-70 (to about 20 Mar), spring 70-160 (to about mid-June), summer 160-255 (to about late Sept), autumn after. If the game wants weather to follow Caesar's lived experience rather than the calendar, extend cold and rain to about day 135 (Avaricum). **Inferred.**

### 3.3 Other game content

- **intro.txt**: "This winter, Caesar is in Italy, and the snow is deep on the passes... an oath over their gathered war standards." Matches VII.1-2 and VII.6-8. **Confirmed.** "Last night a rider came through the hills shouting the news from Cenabum": Caesar describes the news passed by shouting, not by a rider (VII.3). A rider is still plausible for a remote Cadurcan hill fort further from the shouting chain. The Cadurci are about 400 km from Cenabum, so the news would have reached them in about a day by Caesar's method, or two or three by horse. **Fine as written.**
- **Wolves on days 0-110, Roman foragers from day 26** (world.json). With the corrected clock, Roman troops don't reach anywhere near the Cadurci until Caesar garrisons the Province around day 44-50, and those garrisons sit among the Ruteni of the Province and around Narbo. Roman foragers in Cadurcan country before day 44 have no historical basis. Moving them to about day 45-55, as Province patrols, is safer. Strictly, Roman armies operated in Cadurcan territory only in 51 (Rebilus, Fabius, Caesar). **Inferred.**
- **calendar.json "gergovia_taken" day 5.** Rename or reword: Vercingetorix's expulsion and comeback, not a siege (VII.4).
- **calendar.json "cevennes" day 25, "scorched_earth" 50, "avaricum_fallen" 85, "gergovia_victory" 120.** Re-time per 3.1.
- **DESIGN.md: "Central Gaul, from late January to late September".** With R&R the span is about 9 January to 19 October Julian. With Bloch-Carcopino, about late January to early November. Either way, "late September" is too early for Alesia's end, except on Brunaux's dating. Suggest: "from midwinter to mid-autumn 52 BC".
- **DESIGN.md: "the spring assembly is about 120 days away".** There was no spring assembly in Caesar. The Bibracte council was in summer, about day 215 (VII.63). As an invented in-game deadline it's fine, but don't tie it to Bibracte.
- **DESIGN.md: Avaricum "sacked around day 85".** About day 137. Noviodunum "seized by the Aedui around day 130": about day 196.
- **brig_vellaunos.dlg calls Noviodunum "Aedui country".** **Correct** for the Noviodunum of VII.55. Be careful not to confuse it with Noviodunum of the Bituriges (VII.12), which Caesar took in about day 105.
- **oltis_ford.json: the river "brown with snowmelt".** Plausible for the Lot in late winter or spring. **Fine.**
