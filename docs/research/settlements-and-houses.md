# Settlements, houses and fortifications: late La Tène Gaul, southwest and the Cadurci first

Research track 2 of 4. Period: La Tène D (c. 150-30 BC), aimed at 52 BC. Written for the people building the maps and Blender models. Confidence labels: **attested** (an ancient text or an excavation says so), **inferred** (a scholarly reconstruction or my reasoning from evidence), **uncertain/disputed** (with who disputes it). Ancient texts cited by book.chapter; modern Loeb-type translations are paraphrased.

A blunt caveat up front: the Cadurci are thinly excavated. There is good evidence for their ramparts (Murcens, l'Impernal) and for the siege of Uxellodunum, and almost none for what a Cadurci house of 52 BC looked like inside. For houses, this file leans on the nearest well-dug sites (Agen, Vieille-Toulouse, Corent) and on western and central Gaul syntheses, and says so each time.

---

## 1. Summary: what matters most for the game

1. **The rampart is the most wrong thing in the game.** A real murus gallicus was a wall-and-bank several metres high and roughly 10-15 m thick at the base, with a vertical stone face studded with beam ends, a sloping earth ramp behind, and a ditch in front. At Murcens it is still about 5 m high and 12-15 m wide at the base. The game's rampart is lower than a house wall and one tile thick. (Attested: Caesar BG VII.23; Izac & Vaginay; Mercadier & Buchsenschutz.)

2. **"A rampart of timber and limestone" is right for the Cadurci.** The two securely dated Cadurci oppida, Murcens and l'Impernal, both have a murus gallicus with iron spikes (fiches) through crossed timbers and limestone facing. Murcens is the southernmost murus gallicus known. (Attested.)

3. **Quercy forts used cliffs, not full rings.** Murcens has a rampart on the north and west and cliffs on the south and east; the small Quercy sites (under 5 ha) sit on natural spurs. The usual shape is a barrage wall across the neck of a promontory, with cliff edges doing the rest. A closed ring of wall on open ground is the wrong silhouette. (Attested for Murcens; inferred as the general pattern.)

4. **Houses were rectangular timber-framed buildings with wattle-and-daub walls, steep thatch or oak-shingle roofs and earth floors.** Round houses are not the norm in late La Tène Gaul; quadrangular plans dominate. The excavated Quercy example (Montfaucon, Causse de Gramat, earlier La Tène) is a post-built rectangle of about 11.3 x 5 m. (Attested.) The game's rectangular houses are fine.

5. **Low stone footings, yes; stone houses, no.** In the southwest, walls sat on posts, sill beams or low dry-stone or limestone footings (Murcens "solin", Agen limestone radiers, Corent basalt footings). Full stone buildings are not attested before the conquest. The Vindomagos "salt store of real stone" should become timber and daub on a stone footing, or a raised granary. (Attested footings; inferred correction.)

6. **Inside, people sat and slept low.** Posidonius, Diodorus and Strabo all describe Gauls eating seated on hay, straw or skins at low tables, and most sleeping on the ground or on straw. The Cadurci were famous for linen and for mattresses (Pliny NH XIX.2). The chair-height table and bed-frame in the game are wrong; a low table, skins and a straw-and-linen pallet are right. (Attested.)

7. **The hearth was a flat clay slab on the floor, not a ring of stones.** Excavated hearths are slightly sunk clay plates, often laid over amphora sherds or crushed stone, with firedogs, cauldrons and spits. No chimneys. (Attested: Corent, Agen, western Gaul synthesis; Diodorus V.28.)

8. **Roof pitch should go up.** Thatch needs at least about 45 degrees to shed water; 45-55 degrees is normal. The game's 42 degrees is a touch flat. Hip roofs are consistent with the post layouts. Vitruvius and Hirtius both point to oak shingles as well as thatch in Aquitania and at Uxellodunum. (Attested materials; inferred pitch.)

9. **Forty houses does not fit the drawn fort.** Brigodunon's circuit is about 48 x 33 m (0.15 ha). Forty houses with yards, granaries and lanes need something like 2-4 ha. Keep the text and treat the map as a slice, or cut the number. (Inferred.)

10. **The causses in 52 BC were grazed grassland and downy-oak and box scrub, without the dry-stone field walls and corbelled shepherds' huts (cazelles, gariottes) of postcard Quercy.** Those are mostly 18th-19th century. (Attested for the date of the huts and walls; Iron Age vegetation inferred, since no Quercy pollen data were found.)

## 2. Findings by topic

### 2.1 House form: what the texts say

**Strabo IV.4.3** (Posidonius behind it): Gallic houses are large and "dome-shaped" (tholoeideis), built of planks and wicker, with a heavy thatched roof. Most Gauls sleep on the ground, and they eat sitting on beds of straw. Attested as a Greek observer's description. The "dome-shaped" word fits round houses or high, steep roofs; it is the single strongest text for a rounded look, and it conflicts with the excavated rectangular plans of the 2nd-1st c. BC. Treat as **disputed**: probably a general or dated (Posidonius wrote c. 100-90 BC, from travels around Massalia and the Narbonensis fringe) impression, or a description of the roof mass rather than the plan.

**Vitruvius II.1.4**: in Gaul, Hispania, Lusitania and Aquitania, roofs are made "scandulis robusteis aut stramentis", oak shingles or thatch. Attested, and it names Aquitania specifically. **Vitruvius II.1.5**: at Massilia, roofs of earth kneaded with straw rather than tiles (a Mediterranean-fringe detail, not Quercy).

**Caesar BG V.43**: Roman winter-camp huts were thatched "in the Gallic manner" with straw, and the Nervii set them alight with red-hot clay sling bullets and darts. Attested: thatch was the Gallic default, and it burned.

**Hirtius BG VIII.5**: Caesar's troops at Cenabum in winter 52/51 BC were billeted partly in Gaulish houses and partly in huts thatched quickly. Attested.

**Hirtius BG VIII.42**, Uxellodunum (a Cadurci town, 51 BC): the defenders rolled down barrels (cupae) filled with tallow, pitch and wooden shingles (scandulae), set on fire. Attested. This is direct evidence for barrels and shingles in a Cadurci oppidum. It does not prove shingle roofs (the shingles may have been kindling), but with Vitruvius it makes shingle roofs a defensible choice. Inferred.

**Caesar BG VI.30**: Gauls usually build near woods and rivers to avoid the heat. Attested as Caesar's generalisation. **BG V.12**: British buildings are numerous and closely like Gallic ones. Attested.

**Caesar BG I.5**: the Helvetii burn 12 oppida, about 400 vici (villages) and their individual aedificia (farmsteads). Attested; it gives the three-tier hierarchy of fort, village and farm. (Cited from memory of the text, not re-fetched for this file.)

**Strabo IV.4.5**: Gauls hung enemies' heads from their horses' necks and nailed them up at their doorways; Posidonius says he saw it often and got used to it. Attested as an eyewitness claim.

### 2.2 House form: excavated evidence

**Plan and size.** Maguer & Robert (2024) for western and central Gaul: late La Tène houses are mostly quadrangular, on four, six or more posts; ordinary rural houses are about 30-70 m², a few exceed 100 m². Attested. An INRAP late La Tène farm in the Aube has a main building of 135-190 m²; a building at Caen-Beaulieu measures 13 x 10.6 m. Attested. For Quercy, Beausoleil et al. (2006) excavated at Montfaucon on the Causse de Gramat a post-built rectangular building about 11.30 x 5 m (about 56 m²), of the 4th-early 3rd c. BC, inside a partial enclosure trench. The authors call it unique for its period in southwest France. Attested, but earlier than 52 BC and a single site.

**Round houses?** Not in late La Tène Gaul as a norm. Maguer & Robert and the Corent team both stress rectangular buildings; Corent's site guide says explicitly they were not simple round thatched huts. Round houses are the British and Atlantic-fringe type (Butser's reconstructions are British). Attested for the dominance of rectangles; the absence of round houses in Quercy cannot be proved from so few sites. Uncertain for Quercy specifically, inferred against.

**Walls.** Wattle on posts or on a sill beam, daubed with clay (Maguer & Robert; Vieille-Toulouse; Corent). Attested. Where clay was poor, dry stone could take its place in footings (Maguer & Robert). At Vieille-Toulouse walls stood on earth bases stiffened with amphora sherds or on a low sill beam, with posts rarer. Attested. At Agen-Ermitage, buildings sat on limestone radiers (rubble footings) with postholes, and amphora necks were used to wedge posts. Attested. At Murcens, an outer dwelling had "un solin de mur" (a low wall footing) and fragments of raw earth (daub). Attested. Corent: earth and timber on basalt footings, sill beams and posts. Attested.

**Roofs.** No late La Tène roof has survived. Pitch and shape are inferred from post plans: four-pitched (hip) roofs or gabled roofs (Maguer & Robert). Inferred. Vieille-Toulouse: no evidence for roofing material; fired tiles only from the mid-1st c. BC, under Roman influence. Attested. Thatch and oak shingles: attested in texts (above). Reconstructors and Butser agree thatch needs a pitch of at least about 45 degrees; 45-55 degrees is traditional. Inferred from practice.

**Doors.** Entrances are marked by paired posts in the plan (Maguer & Robert). Attested as postholes; the door leaf itself is inferred (planks on wooden pivots or hinges). No chimneys. Smoke left through the thatch or a smoke hole at the gable or hip apex. Inferred, near-universal reconstruction.

**Floors.** Beaten earth (Gergovie craft quarter; Maguer & Robert). Attested. Some floors raised with clay or gravel; Corent paths of amphora sherds. Attested.

**Interior partitions.** Murcens: a roughly square dwelling divided by partitions set on post packings, with storage areas and a reused hearth. Attested (Mercadier & Buchsenschutz 1991). Vieille-Toulouse: internal divisions only from the mid-1st c. BC. Attested. Upper floors: probably lofts for storage or smoking only (Maguer & Robert). Inferred. Corent's main buildings may have had two storeys. Inferred by the excavators.

**Hearths.** On the floor, central or under the highest part of the roof, sometimes with stake holes for spits (Maguer & Robert). Attested. Corent: hearths in the main room, slightly sunk, a clay sole laid over amphora sherds or crushed stone, with hearth plates and firedogs. Attested. Agen: a clay-lined hearth. Attested. Diodorus V.28.4: hearths heaped with coals, with cauldrons and spits loaded with meat. Attested.

**Furnishings.**
Posidonius in Athenaeus IV.151e-152b: the Celts put hay on the ground and eat at wooden tables raised only a little off it, sitting in a circle. Attested. Diodorus V.28.4: they dine seated on the ground on wolf or dog skins. Attested. Strabo IV.4.3: most sleep on the ground and eat on straw beds. Attested. Pliny NH XIX.2 (modern §§7-13): Cadurci linen is valued for mattresses (culcitae); the Gauls invented flock stuffing (tomenta). Attested, and a fine local detail. Iron firedogs, cauldrons, chains and spits are standard finds of the period. Attested in general. Wooden furniture itself almost never survives; any specific form (bench, chest, loom frame) is inferred.

**Storage.** Corent: wood-lined cellars 1-2 m wide, one large rock-cut cellar; four-post granaries in the yards. Attested. Maguer & Robert: four-post granaries and silos are standard on farms. Attested. Barrels: Hirtius VIII.42 (cupae at Uxellodunum). Attested. Iron hoops are rare for this period; barrels were usually bound with wooden withies or split hoops. Inferred. Wine came in Italian amphorae (Dressel 1A), found by the thousand at Murcens and the Puy d'Issolu. Attested.

**Courtyard.** A household unit was a house, outbuildings, granaries and a yard, often fenced with a light palisade or wattle (Corent; Maguer & Robert). Attested.

### 2.3 The southwest and Quercy, site by site

**Murcens (Cras, Lot).** About 80 ha. Murus gallicus on the north and west sides, cliffs on the south and east. The wall is 12-15 m wide at its base and still about 5 m high, followed for about 2 km; timber lacing fixed with iron spikes, clay-filled caissons, beams in quincunx, a rear glacis of big stones; a U-shaped ditch about 2 m deep. Dated late 2nd to mid-1st c. BC. Ore roasting and a possible forge about 20 m behind the wall. Huge imports of Italian wine amphorae: a central place for the wine trade. Abandoned when Divona (Cahors) was founded. Attested (Izac & Vaginay; Mercadier & Buchsenschutz; Büchsenschütz & Mercadier 1989).

**L'Impernal (Luzech, Lot).** About 10-15 ha in a meander of the Lot. Three ramparts of different dates: an Early Iron Age burnt (vitrified) rampart and a murus gallicus with iron spikes, among others. Human skeletons and skulls in three of the murus gallicus caissons. Ironworking; a Late Iron Age level under a later Gallo-Roman fanum; continuous occupation. Attested (Izac & Vaginay; Isnardon; Viré 1913).

**Puy d'Issolu (Vayrac, Lot).** About 80 ha plateau. No clear Late Iron Age rampart; dry-stone walls of uncertain date. Almost no Late Iron Age finds on the plateau itself. But at the Fontaine de Loulié below it: more than 1,000 Italic amphora fragments, more than 700 arrowheads, about 70 catapult bolts and burnt layers. This is widely accepted (Girault and others; officially recognised) as the siege of Uxellodunum. Izac & Vaginay call the settlement itself a minor place. **Disputed**: the siege site is well supported; whether the plateau was a major Cadurci oppidum is not.

**Les Césarines (Saint-Jean-Lespinasse).** A huge dry-stone wall, 12.5 m at the base and 7-12 m high in older accounts, 580 m long, with a ditch. Izac & Vaginay say its murus gallicus is not fully established; Viré thought parts were early medieval. **Uncertain.** Do not use as a model.

**Smaller Cadurci sites** (Brengues, Capdenac, La Roque, Pech del Castel): Late Iron Age finds, defences generally under 5 ha on natural heights. Attested (Izac & Vaginay). These are the closest real equivalents to a "small hill fort" like Brigodunon.

**Pre-Roman Cahors.** No trace of Late Iron Age occupation (Izac & Vaginay). Attested negative. Divona is an Augustan foundation.

**Le Mas-de-Noyer.** I could not find any published site of this name. Unresolved.

**Uxellodunum in Hirtius (BG VIII.32-44).** A town strongly defended by nature, on very steep rocks, formerly under Lucterius's patronage; a river runs through the valley below; a great spring rises at the foot of the town wall, and the Romans finally cut its veins by tunnelling. Attested. Useful for the look of a Cadurci stronghold: cliffs, a river, a spring at the wall foot.

**Agen, Ermitage (Nitiobriges, Lot-et-Garonne).** About 60 ha. A massive clay bank about 4 m high in two phases on a limestone-block base, with no timber lacing or nails, so not a murus gallicus; the ditch was quarried for it. An internal road or esplanade 30-40 m wide surfaced with limestone, a rutted carriageway 15 m wide. Buildings on limestone footings with posts; a clay-lined hearth. Parcel ditches for fields and gardens inside the walls. About 100 wells, one oak-plank-lined well more than 8 m deep. Main occupation 1st c. BC; Roman siege evidence (sling bullets). Attested (Prodeo 2017).

**Vieille-Toulouse (Volcae Tectosages).** Enclosure about 190-200 ha, 6.7 km of V-shaped and double ditches 7-9 m wide and about 3 m deep with a marl bank. Quadrangular houses on earth or sill-beam bases with daub. Orthogonal streets from about 130-120 BC, one road 10 m wide. Wells over 16 m deep in yards. Forges and 17 kilns. Tiles only from the mid-1st c. BC. Attested (Gardes et al. 2022).

**La Sioutat (Roquelaure, Gers).** Italian-style houses with painted plaster, but dated to the last quarter of the 1st c. BC. Attested; too late for 52 BC.

**Corent (Arverni, Puy-de-Dôme).** A regular grid of house plots, at least ten domestic units; main buildings of earth and timber on posts and sill beams, basalt footings, possibly two storeys; sunk clay hearths with firedogs; wood-lined cellars; four-post granaries in the yards; light palisades round the yards; amphora-sherd paths; a covered market hall about 20 m long. Attested (LUERN site publications). A good model for Gergovia-region scenes.

**Answer to "was stone used?":** for footings, paving, rampart facings and well linings, yes, plentifully in limestone country. For whole walls of houses, no secure pre-conquest example in the southwest. Attested footings; full stone houses inferred absent.

### 2.4 Fortifications

**Murus gallicus (Caesar BG VII.23).** Caesar describes it at Avaricum: beams laid on the ground at right angles to the line of the wall, at regular gaps of about 2 feet, tied together on the inside and packed with earth; the gaps on the front filled with big stones; more courses laid on top, alternating so beams never sit directly on beams; beams usually about 40 feet long. He praises it: stone protects against fire and timber against the ram. Attested. Archaeology adds iron spikes 20-30 cm long nailing the crossings (Murcens, l'Impernal, Bibracte), a vertical stone outer face with the beam ends showing, and a sloping earth ramp behind. Attested.

**Dimensions.** Murcens 12-15 m wide at the base, 5 m preserved. Châteaumeillant: beams 0.6-0.8 m apart, stone facing about 0.5 m thick, at least 4-6 m high, about 10 m wide. Attested (Krausz 2006-07). Breastwork on top: a timber parapet or palisade is the standard reconstruction. Inferred; the parapet never survives.

**Fécamp type.** A massive earth bank with a wide flat-bottomed ditch, defined by Wheeler (1957) in Normandy; ditches 5-10 m wide there. At Châteaumeillant a later Fécamp-like rampart with a ditch 40-45 m wide and 3 m deep, built hastily, perhaps around 52 BC. At Bibracte the murus gallicus was later buried under a massive bank (post-conquest). Attested. Not documented in Quercy; northern and central type. Inferred absent from Quercy.

**Southern ramparts.** In the southwest the local tradition was massive dump banks (Agen: clay over limestone blocks, no timber lacing) and huge ditch systems (Vieille-Toulouse). Mediterranean-style stone walls with towers belong to the Languedoc and Provence, not Quercy. Attested for Agen and Vieille-Toulouse. Murcens and l'Impernal are the exception: northern murus gallicus technique pushed to its southern limit.

**Ditches.** Murcens: U-shaped, about 2 m deep. Vieille-Toulouse: V-shaped, 7-9 m wide, about 3 m deep. Châteaumeillant: up to 40-45 m wide. Attested. Where cliffs are steep, no ditch.

**Gates.** The typical late La Tène gate is an inturned gate (Zangentor): the rampart ends turn inward to form a long corridor, with the timber gate at its inner end, so attackers are exposed on three sides. Bibracte's Porte du Rebout is a corridor about 19 m wide, the widest known; the excavators found no tower over the passage. Attested (Buchsenschutz et al. 1998). Note the reconstructed Porte du Rebout was rebuilt in 2022: what visitors see is a restitution. Gates at Quercy sites are not well published. Inferred that the same type was used.

**Is "timber and limestone" right?** Yes, for Murcens and l'Impernal. Attested. For a small fort under 5 ha, a simpler stone-faced bank or a timber palisade on a bank would also be plausible. Inferred.

**Farmstead vs oppidum.** Farms were enclosed by a ditch with a bank or fence and a gate, enough to keep stock in and mark property, not to stand a siege (Maguer & Robert; Montfaucon's enclosure trench). Attested. Oppida had the full rampart, ditch and gate. Attested.

**Palisades and fences.** Palisades: lines of posts set in a trench, attested under the Bibracte rampart and around yards at Corent. Attested. Fences: wattle hurdles (woven withies between stakes) are the normal reconstruction for yards, pens and gardens. Inferred; they leave only stake holes. Post-and-rail fencing of the game's type needs sawn rails and is a later look. Inferred.

### 2.5 Settlement types and scale

**Hierarchy.** Oppida (large fortified centres), vici (open villages or agglomerations), aedificia (farms). Attested (Caesar BG I.5; BG VI.43). Haselgrove & Guichard: open agglomerations often came before the oppida, oppida were often short-lived, and rural settlement thinned in La Tène D2. Inferred.

**What "a small hill fort of forty houses" looks like.** Quercy's small defended sites are under 5 ha (Izac & Vaginay). Attested. At household plots of the Corent type (a house, a granary, a yard), say 400-800 m² each including lanes, forty households need roughly 2-4 ha. Inferred. Layout: one or two lanes from the gate, houses in fenced yards, granaries and pits, a forge near the wall (Murcens), wells or a spring, open spaces for stock. Inferred from Corent, Agen and Murcens.

**Sanctuaries.** Late La Tène sanctuaries in Gaul are typically a ditched or palisaded square enclosure with offering pits, weapons, animal bones; the stone temple (fanum) and altar are Gallo-Roman. At l'Impernal a Late Iron Age level lies under a later fanum. Attested. Human remains built into the l'Impernal rampart caissons. Attested; their meaning is unclear.

**Crafts.** Iron smelting and forging (Murcens, l'Impernal); pottery kilns (Vieille-Toulouse); linen weaving (Strabo IV.2.2 for the Cadurci; Pliny NH XIX.2). Attested. Rotary querns were standard in the period. Attested in general; I have no good Quercy-specific publication.

**Water.** Wells: Agen about 100, plank-lined, more than 8 m; Vieille-Toulouse more than 16 m. Springs at the foot of the wall: Uxellodunum (BG VIII.40-41). Attested. On limestone plateaux, water is the hard problem; this suits the game.

**Roads.** Agen internal road 30-40 m with a rutted 15 m carriageway; Vieille-Toulouse a 10 m road; Bibracte main road about 14 m. Attested. Rural tracks are inferred.

**Fields.** Parcels marked by ditches, even inside enclosures (Agen). Attested. Crops: barley, millet, naked wheat, emmer, spelt, pulses; millet well documented in the southwest. Attested broadly; nothing Quercy-specific found.

### 2.6 The causses landscape

**Vegetation.** Today: downy oak (Quercus pubescens) with hornbeam, Montpellier maple, box (Buxus sempervirens), Mediterranean buckthorn, phillyrea and terebinth; dry limestone grasslands kept open by grazing. Attested for the present (Boulet, PNR Causses du Quercy). In the Iron Age, a grazed mosaic of grassland, juniper and box scrub and oak woods is likely. Inferred; I found no Iron Age pollen data for Quercy.

**Farming.** Sheep and pigs on the plateaux, cereals in the valley bottoms and dolines. Strabo IV.4.3 notes many pigs and large flocks in Gaul. Attested generally; Quercy specifics inferred.

**Dry-stone walls and huts.** The famous causse field walls appear from the late Middle Ages and mostly date to the 19th century; the corbelled cazelles and gariottes are mostly 18th-19th century. Attested (Lassure; Sénat & Canou; CAUE/DREAL Occitanie). Do not put them in 52 BC.

**Iron Age cave use.** Cave sanctuaries are attested for the neighbouring Ruteni: Rajal del Gorp on the Larzac (water, chthonic deposits, 41 Ruteni silver coins), with others (Sargel, l'Ourtiguet). Attested (Demierre et al. 2015; Gruat). I found no securely published Iron Age cave cult in Quercy itself. The grotto shrine is plausible by analogy. Inferred.

**The Lot / Oltis.** Oltis is given as the ancient (Gallo-Roman) form, with Olt as the Occitan name today and later medieval spellings. I could not verify the exact ancient attestation, and Caesar does not name the river. **Uncertain.** Fine to use; don't build a plot on it.

### 2.7 Winter

Caesar crossed the Cevennes to the Arverni through snow six feet deep (BG VII.8). Attested; that is in the uplands, not the Lot valley. Roman troops wintered in Gaulish houses and quickly thatched huts (BG VIII.5). Attested. Büntgen et al. (2011) reconstruct warm, wet summers in the Roman period, but for central Europe and summers only. Uncertain for Quercy winters. Practical inference for art: thatch heavy with wet or snow, smoke seeping through roofs, stock inside or close by, frozen mud and ruts. Inferred.

## 3. Check against the game

Ordered from most wrong to least.

### 3.1 Rampart (art/sprites.py `rampart`, brigodunon.json "R") — wrong

Current: about 1.25 high plus a 0.6-0.75 stake breastwork, 1.15 wide, beam ends on both faces, a closed ring round the fort. Total about 1.9 units, under the house wall plus eave.

Evidence: Murcens 12-15 m wide and still 5 m high; Châteaumeillant 4-6 m+ high, about 10 m wide (Izac & Vaginay; Krausz). Caesar BG VII.23: beam ends only on the outer face, earth packed behind.

Change: make it clearly taller than any house, at least 3-4 tiles high in the cutaway sense or at least visually twice the house eave, and 3-6 hexes deep. Outer face: vertical limestone with square timber ends in rows, no beam ends on the inner side. Inner side: a sloping earth ramp. Top: a timber breastwork, plank or post. Out front: a ditch, dry on limestone. If full scale breaks the camera, keep the faces honest and cheat the depth. Change the docstring as well: beam ends on both faces is wrong.

### 3.2 Fort plan (brigodunon.json) — wrong silhouette

Current: a closed rectangular ring with a gate gap.

Evidence: Murcens has a wall on two sides and cliffs on two; small Quercy sites sit on natural heights; Uxellodunum on very steep rocks above a river (BG VIII.33, VIII.40).

Change: put Brigodunon on a spur or bend of the river with cliff edges on two or three sides and the rampart as a barrage across the neck. The road climbs to an inturned gate: two rampart arms turned inward, a corridor, the gate at the inner end. No tower over the passage.

### 3.3 Scale: "forty houses" — mismatch

Current: about 0.15 ha drawn, about six buildings, forty houses in the text.

Evidence: small Quercy forts under 5 ha; forty households need 2-4 ha (inferred).

Change: either say "a dozen houses" or make the map visibly a slice of a larger fort (lanes leading off-screen, roofs beyond). Both are honest; the current state is not.

### 3.4 Furniture (props `table`, `bed`) — wrong

Current: chair-height table, a raised bed frame.

Evidence: Athenaeus IV.151e-152b, Diodorus V.28.4, Strabo IV.4.3.

Change: a low wooden table (about 0.3-0.4 m), people seated on hay, straw or skins; beds as straw pallets or a low platform with skins and a linen-covered mattress. Cadurci linen mattresses (Pliny NH XIX.2) are a good local item. No chairs.

### 3.5 Hearth (prop `hearth`) — wrong type

Current: a ring of 11 stones with logs, a campfire.

Evidence: Corent, Agen, Maguer & Robert; Diodorus V.28.4.

Change: a flat, slightly sunk clay plate, maybe with an amphora-sherd edge, a pair of iron firedogs, a cauldron on a chain from the roof beams, a spit. Smoke stains on the roof underside if visible.

### 3.6 Stone salt store at Vindomagos (`wall_stone` "s") — anachronistic

Current: a 5 x 4 building with full stone walls, and text boasting "real stone".

Evidence: no pre-conquest full-stone buildings in the southwest; stone footings common.

Change: timber and daub walls on a knee-high dry-stone footing, or a raised granary on posts. The text can still brag: "a store on a stone footing" or "the only stone-footed store on the salt road". Keep `wall_stone` for footings, wells, rampart faces.

### 3.7 Shrine altar (prop `altar`, brigodunon top) — Roman form

Current: a stone block with a slab, trigger "offering box on the altar stone".

Evidence: late La Tène sanctuaries are ditched or palisaded enclosures with pits; stone altars come later.

Change: a small square enclosure (ditch and palisade or wattle) with an offering pit, a carved wooden post or a tree, weapons or skulls on display. Rename the trigger target (offering pit, sacred post).

### 3.8 Roofs (game/src/view/roofs.gd) — close, adjust

Current: hip roofs, PITCH 42°, thatch texture.

Evidence: hip roofs consistent with post plans (inferred); thatch needs about 45° minimum (inferred practice); oak shingles also attested (Vitruvius II.1.4; BG VIII.42).

Change: raise PITCH to about 48°. Optionally add a shingle variant for the chief's hall. No chimneys; a smoke hole at the hip apex for houses with hearths. Heavy thick eaves are right.

### 3.9 Walls (`wall_wood`) — mostly right

Current: posts and daub panels with studs and a sill.

Evidence: wattle and daub on posts or sill beams (attested everywhere above).

Change: add a low dry-stone footing course (one or two courses of limestone) as an option for Quercy. Daub in pale clay or limewash-grey, not brick-red. The house sizes (about 60 m² for the player's house, about 200 m² for the chief's hall) are consistent with excavated ranges. Confirmed.

### 3.10 Fences and pens (`fence`, Vindomagos "n") — wrong look

Current: posts with two horizontal rails.

Evidence: wattle and palisades (Corent; inferred from stake holes).

Change: woven wattle hurdles between stakes for pens and yards; post palisades for important yards. The `palisade` sprite (pointed stakes, 1.7-2.05) is plausible for a farm or yard enclosure.

### 3.11 Props and dressing — mostly fine, additions

Barrels: confirmed by BG VIII.42; switch iron hoops to wooden hoops. Add Italian wine amphorae (tall, pointed, Dressel 1A) at the chief's hall and a trader's, as the Cadurci drank huge amounts (Murcens, Puy d'Issolu). Add a warp-weighted loom with clay weights for linen (Strabo IV.2.2; Pliny NH XIX.2). Add four-post granaries, storage pits and a rotary quern. Optional and grim: heads nailed at a warrior's doorway (Strabo IV.4.5).

### 3.12 Grotto — plausible, flag the invention

Cave sanctuaries attested among the Ruteni next door (Rajal del Gorp). The deep shrine with bones, an idol and torque is defensible. The dozen hanged dead roped to iron pegs is invention: nothing found matches it. It does not contradict anything known, so it passes the design rule, but don't present it as evidence-based. Human remains in the l'Impernal rampart show the Cadurci did put bodies in odd places.

### 3.13 Confirmations

Vindomagos as an unwalled village with fields to the water: right; open agglomerations were common (Haselgrove & Guichard; BG I.5 vici). Rectangular houses with earth floors and doors on the long side: right. A forge near the wall: matches Murcens. Water from wells or a spring at the foot of the wall: matches Agen and Uxellodunum; worth using. Fields and meadow outside the rampart: right, though Agen also had plots inside. Limestone country, river below: right.

### 3.14 Landscape

No dry-stone field walls or corbelled huts. Use downy oak, box and juniper scrub, short grazed grass, bare limestone, sheep and pigs.
