# Gallic society, religion, daily life, economy, language and names (52 BC)

Research track 4 of 4. Written for the people designing and writing Vae Victis. The Cadurci and the southwest come first wherever the evidence allows; where it doesn't, the nearest good evidence is given and labeled as such.

Confidence labels: **attested** (an ancient text, inscription, coin or excavation says so), **inferred** (scholarly reconstruction or analogy), **uncertain/disputed** (and who disputes it). Ancient sources are cited by book.chapter. Short Latin phrases are quoted from the public-domain texts; everything in modern copyrighted translation is paraphrased.

A standing caveat for everything below: almost all written evidence about Gallic society comes from Greeks and Romans, writing for their own audiences. Caesar wrote propaganda for Rome (book VI's ethnography is a set piece designed to justify conquest, and to contrast Gauls with Germans). Posidonius (c. 100 BC), whose lost work underlies much of Strabo IV, Diodorus V and Athenaeus IV, actually travelled in southern Gaul, but his material is half a century older than 52 BC and centred on the Mediterranean south. Treat every "the Gauls do X" as "a Roman or Greek author says some Gauls did X".

---

## 1. Summary: the findings that matter most for the game

1. **The Cadurci were clients of the Arverni, and their great man was Lucterius.** Caesar groups the Cadurci with the Eleuteti, Gabali and Vellavii as peoples "accustomed to be under the rule of the Arverni" (VII.75). Lucterius is the only named Cadurcan; he struck coins in his own name (silver and bronze, legend LVXTIIRIOS, Latin letters), and his family name survives a century later on two Latin inscriptions of Cadurci aristocrats. Hirtius says the town of Uxellodunum "had been in his clientela" (VIII.32): a whole hill fort could be a noble's client. That is the best available model for how Brigodunon was run. (attested)

2. **A small fort would be run by a patron noble plus its own elders, with druids as judges.** Caesar describes a society of nobles (equites) with followings of clients and ambacti, a commons "almost in the position of slaves" (VI.13, VI.15), factions down to single households (VI.11), and druids who judge murders, inheritances and boundaries (VI.13). He also shows communities acting through their *maiores natu*, their elders (II.13, II.28). Brigodunon's council of elders is plausible; its elders judging a killing on their own is less so (see section 6).

3. **The guest-bond is better attested than the game may realise.** Nicolaus of Damascus (1st century BC) says the Celts punish the killing of a foreigner with death but the killing of a fellow citizen only with exile, and never lock their doors. Diodorus (V.28) says they feed strangers before asking who they are. This supports both the game's "killing a guest is the worst crime" and Dubnacos's exile for killing a neighbour. (attested, with the usual caveat)

4. **Stealing from a shrine is the worst kind of crime the sources record.** Caesar says spoils vowed to the gods lie heaped in consecrated places and nobody dares take from them; the penalty is "gravissimum supplicium cum cruciatu", death by torture (VI.17), and thieves are the preferred human sacrifices (VI.16). Diodorus says sanctuaries were full of untouched gold (V.27). Dubnacos robbing the shrine is far more serious in Gallic terms than in the current dialogue.

5. **The Grotto's "dead hung for Taranis" contradicts the one source that pairs gods with killing methods.** The Berne scholia on Lucan I.445 say victims of *Esus* were hung from trees; Taranis's were burned in a wooden vessel; Teutates's drowned head-first in a vat. Change the god to Esus, or make it a local god with no name the outsiders know.

6. **Wine, salt and silver are right; some details are off.** The Cadurci oppidum at Puy d'Issolud (Uxellodunum) has hundreds of Italian Dressel 1 wine-amphora fragments (Benquet). The southwest's money was the silver "monnaies à la croix", and the Cadurci struck their own. Iron Age Atlantic salt was made by boiling brine in clay troughs (briquetage), not in evaporation pans. Gallic bread was, per Pliny, *lighter* than others because it was leavened with beer froth.

7. **Several names are good, some need a tweak.** Cintugnatos, Cobnertos, Moccos, Iccios, Dannotalos, Ateula and Vindomagos are attested names or very close to them. Vellaunos and Dubnacos are well-formed. **Epona** is a goddess's name; a girl named after a goddess is unattested in Gaul, and the safer form is *Eponina* (attested for a Lingonian noblewoman). Oltis for the Lot is the attested Roman-era name.

8. **Cahors did not exist in 52 BC.** Divona Cadurcorum was founded under Augustus or Tiberius on a new site (Rigal 2009). Where the Cadurci's chief town stood in 52 BC is unknown; the candidates are Murcens, l'Impernal (Luzech) and Uxellodunum (Puy d'Issolud).

9. **Time is counted in nights, and that's usable flavor.** Caesar VI.18: the Gauls measure time "not by the number of days but of nights", and birthdays, months and years start at night. Pliny XVI.250: druids start months, years and a thirty-year age on the sixth day of the moon. The Coligny calendar is 250 years later but can be used with a caveat.

10. **There is almost no attested Gaulish dialogue, so invent, and don't fake attestation.** No greeting formula survives. What survives: oaths on gathered standards (VII.2), a cavalry oath not to go under a roof until the enemy column has been ridden through twice (VII.66), the Gallic habit of stopping travellers to demand news (IV.5), loans repayable in the next world (Valerius Maximus, Mela), the fear that the sky would fall (Strabo VII.3.8), and a handful of words: *nemeton* (sanctuary), *dunon* (fort), *magos* (plain/market), *ritu-* (ford), *ambactos* (servant/client), *corma/curmi* (beer).

## 2. Findings by topic

### 2.1 Society and politics

**Two privileged orders, and everyone else.** Caesar says that in all Gaul only two classes count: druids and *equites* (nobles, "knights"). The commons are held "almost in the place of slaves" (*plebes paene servorum habetur loco*), dare nothing on their own, and are never consulted; many, crushed by debt, taxes or the power of stronger men, give themselves into servitude to nobles, who then hold over them the rights of masters over slaves (VI.13.1-3). **Attested** (Caesar), though the picture is schematic: he is describing the great civitates (Aedui, Arverni) and he has a reason to make Gaul look like an oligarchy in need of order.

**Clientage is the measure of a man.** "The greater a man's birth and wealth, the more *ambacti* and clients he has around him; this is the one kind of influence and power they recognise" (VI.15.2). *Ambactus* is a Gaulish word (a retainer, servant; it is the ancestor of English "embassy" via Latin). **Attested.** In the southwest there is a striking variant: the **soldurii** of the Sotiates (Aquitanian neighbours of the Cadurci to the southwest), sworn companions who share all good things with their lord in life and die with him; Caesar says none had ever been known to refuse death when his lord fell (III.22, the 600 devoted men of Adiatuanus). **Attested** for the Sotiates; whether the Cadurci had the same institution is unknown.

**Factions everywhere.** Not only every civitas and every *pagus* (district) but nearly every household is split into factions, whose leaders are the men judged to have the most authority; Caesar says the old purpose was that no common man should lack help against a more powerful one (VI.11.2-4). Whole civitates were divided the same way, with the Aedui and Sequani (later the Remi) leading rival blocs, and weaker peoples as clients of stronger (VI.12). **Attested.**

**Peoples as clients of peoples.** The Cadurci, Eleuteti, Gabali and Vellavii are listed with the Arverni as peoples "qui sub imperio Arvernorum esse consuerunt" (VII.75.2). For the relief army of Alesia, the Arverni and these clients together were asked for 35,000 men (VII.75.2, "parem numerum" with the Aedui). Pailler (2012) treats Cadurci clientship to the Arverni as long-standing. **Attested** (Caesar); the depth of the dependency is **inferred**.

**Lucterius and the Cadurci in 52-51 BC.** Lucterius the Cadurcan, "a man of the greatest daring" (*summae hominem audaciae*, VII.5.1), is sent by Vercingetorix to the Ruteni, wins them over, takes hostages from the Nitiobriges and Gabali, and threatens the Province toward Narbo (VII.5, VII.7-8). Later the Ruteni and Cadurci are ordered to ravage the Volcae Arecomici (VII.64.6). In 51 BC he joins Drappes the Senonian, who had recruited freed slaves, exiles "from all the states" and brigands (VIII.30.1), and they occupy Uxellodunum, "which had been in his clientela" (*quod in clientela fuerat eius*, VIII.32.2). He escapes (VIII.35), is handed over in chains by the pro-Roman Arvernian Epasnactus (VIII.44.3); Caesar cuts off the hands of every defender of Uxellodunum who bore arms (VIII.44.1). **Attested.**

Lucterius's coins: two small series carry his name, LVXTIIRIOS / LVXTIIPIOS / LVXTERIOS, in Latin letters (sometimes with a Greek-shaped rho): silver "à la croix" pieces of about 1.3 g (seven known, three from the Lot, two at Luzance about 12 km from Puy d'Issolud) and small bronzes with a head and a galloping horse (finds from the Oise to the Ariège, including Gergovia). The double II is the Gaulish way of writing a long e. Pailler concludes that Lucterius or a close relative was the Cadurci's ruler at the time of the war. The name survives as a Roman family name, apparently unknown outside Cadurci country: Marcus Lucterius Leo, son of Lucterius Senecianus, "having held all honours in his homeland", priest of the Altar of Augustus at Lugdunum, honoured by the *civitas Cadurcorum* (CIL XIII 1541 = ILS 7041, Cahors; and AE 1955, 212, Lyon), probably 1st century AD. Pailler glosses the name as "wrestler/fighter", comparing Old Irish *luchtaire*. **Attested** (coins, inscriptions); the etymology is **inferred**.

**Magistrates, senates, the vergobret.** Among the Aedui the chief magistrate is the *vergobretus*, elected annually, with power of life and death over his people (I.16.5). In 52 BC Caesar settles a disputed election: Aeduan law forbade two members of one family from holding the office or sitting in the senate while both lived, and the legitimate magistrate was the one chosen "through the priests, according to the custom of the state" (*per sacerdotes more civitatis*, VII.33.4). The title also appears outside the Aedui: on Lexovian coins (CISIAMBOS CATTOS VERCOBRETO) and a Saintes inscription (from memory; verify in RIG IV / CIL XIII before citing in game text). Senates are attested for the Aedui (VII.32-33), Nervii (II.28) and Veneti (III.16). **Attested** for those peoples. **For the Cadurci we have no word on magistrates at all**; a vergobret-like annual magistrate is a plausible invention, not a fact.

**Kingship was dangerous.** Vercingetorix's father Celtillus was killed by his own civitas for seeking kingship (VII.4.1); Orgetorix of the Helvetii was put on trial for the same (I.4); Vercingetorix is hailed king by his followers (VII.4.5). Teutomatus son of Ollovico is "king of the Nitiobriges", the Cadurci's western neighbours (VII.31.5). **Attested.** So both kings and aristocratic councils coexist in 52 BC; the southwest had at least one king next door.

**Elders.** Caesar repeatedly shows communities acting through their *maiores natu* when nobles are absent: the elders of the Bellovaci come out of Bratuspantium to surrender (II.13.2); the elders of the Nervii send envoys after the battle (II.28.1). **Attested.** An elders' council governing a small fort, under the patronage of a noble (as Uxellodunum was under Lucterius), is a solid **inference**.

**Assemblies and rumour.** "Well-governed" civitates have a law that anyone who hears news about public affairs from neighbours, by rumour or report, must take it to the magistrate and tell no one else; the magistrates decide what to hide and what to publish, and public affairs may only be discussed in council (VI.20). Nicolaus of Damascus (F5, via Stobaeus 4.2.25) says the Celts conduct all public business armed. Caesar's *concilium armatum*: by law all adult men come armed, and the last to arrive is tortured to death in front of the rest (V.56.2, the Treveri). Caesar himself held his *concilium Galliae* "at the start of spring" (VI.3.4). **Attested.** The game's "spring assembly" of the Cadurci is plausible.

**How a small Cadurci hill fort would be run (inference, for designers).** A patron: a Cadurci noble (in 52 BC possibly of Lucterius's following) whose clients the people of the fort are, who takes their dues and leads their men to war. A council of elders (heads of families) handling daily matters: fields, herds, the stores, quarrels short of bloodshed. A druid, or a vates acting for one, presiding over sacrifice and giving judgment in killings and inheritance disputes, perhaps referring hard cases to the annual druidic assembly in Carnute territory (VI.13.10). The commons, many of them in debt to someone. Some slaves. A smith who is valued. None of this is documented for a specific Cadurci site; it is assembled from Caesar plus Hirtius VIII.32.

### 2.2 Hostages

**Practice.** Caesar takes hostages after almost every submission: from the Helvetii (I.14), Bellovaci (600, II.15), the Treveran Indutiomarus (200, including his son and all his relatives, V.4.2), the Senones (100, handed to the Aedui to keep, VI.4.4), and so on. Gauls did it to each other too: the Sequani held the children of Aeduan nobles and made them swear never to ask for them back (I.31.7, VI.12.4); Ariovistus demanded "the children of all the noblest" and inflicted every torture on them (I.31.12); Lucterius took hostages from the Nitiobriges and Gabali (VII.7.2); Vercingetorix demanded hostages from every civitas (VII.4.7, VII.64.1). **Attested.**

**Who.** Almost always the children and close kin of nobles (*principum liberi*, *nobilissimi cuiusque liberi*). They are given by a civitas or a leader, not by a village. **Attested** pattern.

**Where kept.** In the custody of an allied civitas (the Senones' hostages with the Aedui, VI.4.4) or at a Roman depot: in 52 BC Caesar had "all the hostages of Gaul", grain, public money and much baggage at Noviodunum on the Loire; the Aedui killed the guards and traders there and took the hostages to Bibracte (VII.55.2-5). **Attested.** The game's Noviodunum is exactly right.

**Treatment.** Caesar does not describe it. The nearest analogy for Roman education of hostages is Sertorius in Spain, who gathered the sons of Iberian nobles at Osca and had them taught Greek and Latin, which made them hostages in fact (Plutarch, *Sertorius* 14). "Raised Roman, or tries" is therefore a reasonable **inference**, not a documented Gallic case.

### 2.3 Guest and host

- Nicolaus of Damascus, *Collection of Customs*, F5 (Stobaeus 4.2.25), on the Celts: killing a foreigner is punished more harshly than killing a citizen, the first with death, the second with exile; they never lock their houses. **Attested** (one compiler, 1st century BC, probably from Posidonius or earlier ethnography).
- Diodorus V.28.5: they invite strangers to their feasts and only after the meal ask who they are and what they need. **Attested.**
- Caesar IV.5.2: it is a Gallic custom to make travellers stop, even against their will, and ask what each has heard; in towns crowds surround merchants and make them say where they come from and what news they bring; and they then act on rumours they later regret. **Attested.** This is the social engine behind every "What's the news from the road?" in the game.
- Posidonius in Athenaeus IV.36-40 (151e-154c): feasts on hay or skins at low tables; the strongest or most noble sits in the middle like a chorus-leader; shield-bearers stand behind, spearmen sit opposite; drink from a common cup, a little at a time but often; in former times the bravest took the thigh joint, and if another claimed it they fought to the death; the Arvernian king Louernios (Luernios) showing off by scattering gold and silver from his chariot and holding a feast in a vast enclosure, and tossing a bag of gold to a late-arriving poet who sang that the very tracks of his chariot sprouted gold and blessings. **Attested** (Posidonius's eyewitness-era material, c. 100 BC; the Louernios story is about the 120s BC).
- Germanic hospitality is a separate, explicit Roman topos: Caesar VI.23.9, to violate a guest is unlawful; Tacitus *Germania* 21. Useful for Haldo's men.

The game's Honor rule ("killing a host or a guest under your roof is the worst crime") is well supported. Strictly, the sources speak of killing a *stranger/foreigner* rather than a guest specifically; the difference doesn't matter for gameplay.

### 2.4 Women

- **Marriage and property.** Husbands put into a common fund an amount of their own goods equal to the dowry they received; the joint fund and its income are kept, and the survivor takes both shares (VI.19.1-2). **Attested.**
- **Power of life and death.** Husbands have it over wives as over children; when a noble dies in suspicious circumstances his relatives examine the wives under torture like slaves and, if guilt is found, burn them (VI.19.3). **Attested** (Caesar; part of a contrast with Rome, so treat with care).
- **Marriage politics.** Dumnorix married his mother to the most powerful man among the Bituriges, married himself to a Helvetian, and married his half-sister and other kinswomen into other civitates (I.18.6-7); Orgetorix gave his daughter to Dumnorix (I.3.5). **Attested.** Women of noble families were alliance currency.
- **Public role.** Women appeal from the walls at Avaricum and Gergovia (VII.26.3, VII.47.5-48.3). Diodorus says Gallic women match their men in stature and courage (V.32.2). Plutarch (*Mulierum virtutes* 6) tells that Celtic women once arbitrated a quarrel between their menfolk so well that a treaty with Hannibal made Celtic women judges of complaints against the Carthaginians. Mela (III.48) describes nine virgin priestesses on the island of Sena (Sein) with power over winds and seas. Tacitus says the Britons "make no distinction of sex in their commanders" (*Agricola* 16.1). **Attested** for each author; how far any of it applies to the Cadurci is **uncertain**.
- **Attested women of rank.** In Gaul itself almost none are named in Caesar. Later: Eponina (Epponina), wife of the Lingonian rebel Julius Sabinus, AD 69-79 (Tacitus *Histories* IV.67; Plutarch *Amatorius* 25). The Larzac lead tablet (c. AD 100, Ruteni country, next door to the Cadurci) names a group of women in a magical dispute, with metronymic forms such as "daughter of" (*duxtir*) and "mother" (*matir*). Galatian women (Chiomara, Camma) are attested in Plutarch and Polybius but belong to Anatolia.
- **A headwoman (Sulicena).** No Gallic source names a woman governing a village. It is not contradicted either, and British queens (Cartimandua, Boudica) show it was conceivable in the Celtic world. **Uncertain** but defensible.

### 2.5 Religion

**Druids, bards and vates.**
- Strabo IV.4.4: three honoured classes: bards (hymn-singers and poets), vates (*ouateis*: sacrificers and natural philosophers), and druids (natural and moral philosophy; most just of men; judges of private and public disputes, especially murder cases; formerly they arbitrated wars and stopped armies about to fight). They teach that souls and the universe are indestructible, though fire and water will at some time prevail. **Attested.**
- Diodorus V.31: bards sing to an instrument like a lyre, praising some and abusing others; philosophers and theologians (druids) are highly honoured; seers (*manteis*) foretell by augury and by sacrifice; no sacrifice happens without a philosopher present; in war, both sides heed them, and they have stepped between battle lines and stopped the fighting. **Attested.**
- Caesar VI.13-14: druids handle sacrifices public and private and interpret religion; young men flock to them; they judge almost all disputes, including killings, inheritance and boundaries, and set rewards and penalties; anyone who defies their decision is barred from sacrifices, the heaviest penalty, after which people shun him and he gets no justice; one chief druid, chosen by vote or by arms; a yearly session in Carnute territory, "reckoned the centre of all Gaul"; the doctrine came from Britain; they don't fight or pay tax; training can last twenty years, all by memorised verse; they don't write their doctrine, though for other public and private accounts Gauls use Greek letters; they teach that souls pass after death to other bodies. **Attested**, and the passage is where Caesar is most suspected of literary shaping (Pailler 2008 on "Caesar's druids").
- Caesar never mentions bards or vates. The game's vates (Cintugnatos as seer, healer, shrine-keeper) fits Strabo and Diodorus. A vates presiding over a small community's shrine, with a druid somewhere above him, is a sound **inference**.
- Mela III.19: druids teach the noblest youths secretly for up to twenty years "in a cave or in remote glades" (*in specu aut in abditis saltibus*). **Attested** (1st century AD). A nice hook for the Grotto.
- Hirtius VIII.38 names a Carnute, Gutruatus, executed as instigator of the revolt; inscriptions show *gutuater* was a priestly title (e.g., "gutuater Martis" at Mâcon), so Hirtius may have turned a title into a name. **Attested** (title in inscriptions; the misreading is **inferred**).

**Mistletoe.** Pliny XVI.249-251: druids hold nothing more sacred than mistletoe growing on an oak (rare); on the sixth day of the moon a priest in white climbs the tree, cuts it with a golden sickle into a white cloak, and two white bulls are sacrificed; they call it "all-healing" and give it in drink to make animals fertile and as a cure for all poisons. **Attested** (Pliny). The game's "druid's poultice" with mistletoe is a reasonable extrapolation; external use on wounds isn't recorded. Pliny also gives druidic plant-gathering rites for *selago* and *samolus* (XXIV.103-104) and the "serpent's egg" (*anguinum*) charm (XXIX.52). Marcellus of Bordeaux (c. AD 400, southwestern) preserves Gaulish plant names and healing charms; late, but local.

**The gods.** Caesar VI.17: Mercury most worshipped (inventor of arts, guide on roads and journeys, most powerful for gain and trade), then Apollo (drives off disease), Mars (war; spoils are vowed to him), Jupiter (rules the heavens), Minerva (crafts). These are Roman labels for unnamed Gallic gods (*interpretatio Romana*). VI.18: all Gauls claim descent from Dis Pater. Lucan I.444-446 names Teutates, Esus and Taranis, appeased by cruel blood. The Berne scholia on that passage (9th-century manuscript, older material) give: Teutates = Mercury, victim plunged head-first into a full vat until he suffocates; Esus = Mars, victim hung on a tree until his limbs fall apart in gore; Taranis = Dis Pater, victims burned in a wooden vessel (*in alveo ligneo*). The scholia also give alternative identifications (Teutates as Mars, Taranis as Jupiter). **Attested** (late scholia of unknown source); **disputed** in value. Modern consensus: these were not a pan-Gallic trinity, and local deities (Lugus, Belenos, Sucellos, Epona, Cernunnos and hundreds of local names) dominate the later inscriptions. Taranis does appear in Roman-period dedications (*Taranucnus*, *Tanarus*), mostly in the east and Germany.

**Sanctuaries and deposits.**
- Gournay-sur-Aronde (Oise, Bellovaci; from the 4th-3rd century BC): a ditched, palisaded enclosure with a central pit about 2 m deep, a "hollow altar", where carcasses of old cattle were left to decompose before their bones went into the ditch; thousands of weapons (swords, scabbards, shields, spears) deposited, many deliberately damaged; human bones too. Brunaux, Méniel and Rapin 1980; Brunaux, Méniel and Poplin 1985. **Attested.**
- Ribemont-sur-Ancre (Somme, Ambiani): a monumental war trophy of the late 3rd century BC, with the headless bodies of several hundred armed men displayed standing, later built into "ossuaries" of long bones; heads absent, presumably taken; no significant cult after, until a Roman temple c. 30 BC. Brunaux et al. 1999 (Gallia 56); radiocarbon and isotope work Clauzel et al. 2023. **Attested.** This is the archaeological reality behind "the victors displayed the dead" (the game's Bone Field).
- Strabo IV.1.13 on Tolosa (Toulouse, the nearest big southwestern sanctuary centre): the treasure of the Tectosages lay untouched in sacred enclosures and sacred lakes; the Romans who took it in 106 BC sold the lakes. Diodorus V.27.4: much gold lies dedicated in temples and sacred precincts and no native touches it from fear of the gods, though the Celts love money. Caesar VI.17.4-5: in many civitates heaps of spoils stand in consecrated places; rarely does anyone dare hide booty or take from the heaps, and the punishment is death by torture. **Attested.**
- Southwestern gold torques were mostly found in hoards and deposits, not graves (Fenouillet, Lasgraïsses, Tayac): from memory, verify in a regional catalogue before using as a specific fact. **Inferred**.

**The head cult.** Diodorus V.29.4-5 and Strabo IV.4.5 (both from Posidonius, who says he saw it and grew used to it): they hang the heads of enemies from their horses' necks and nail them up at the doors of their houses; heads of distinguished enemies are embalmed in cedar oil, kept in chests and shown to guests, and they would not sell one for its weight in gold. Archaeology from the Mediterranean south: Entremont (Saluvii, destroyed 123 BC) with a pillar carved with heads and real skulls with nail holes; Roquepertuse (portico with niches holding skulls, 3rd century BC); Le Cailar (Gard, nailed heads). **Attested** for the south and north; no Cadurci evidence.

**Human sacrifice.** Caesar VI.16: Gauls in grave illness or danger sacrifice humans or vow to; they believe only a life can buy a life; some use huge wicker figures filled with living men and burnt; they prefer those caught in theft or robbery, but use innocents if criminals run short. Strabo IV.4.5 (stab in the back, divine from convulsions; shooting with arrows; impaling in temples; the wicker colossus with cattle and animals), Diodorus V.31.3 (stab above the diaphragm, divine from the fall and the blood), Cicero *Pro Fonteio* 31, Pliny XXX.13 (ended under Tiberius), Suetonius *Claudius* 25 (Claudius abolished the druids' religion). **Attested** in texts; **disputed** in scale and meaning. Archaeology is ambiguous: Ribemont and Gournay are war trophies and offerings, not clear sacrifices; candidates include the Acy-Romance (Ardennes) young men buried crouched in boxes (1st century BC, Lambot), interpreted by some as sacrificial. The Romans had motive to exaggerate.

**Burial in late La Tène.** Caesar VI.19.4: funerals are splendid and costly; everything the dead loved, including animals, goes on the pyre; until recently slaves and favoured clients were burned too. Diodorus V.28.6: letters to the dead are thrown onto the pyre. Mela III.19 and Valerius Maximus II.6.10: they lend money to be repaid in the next world. Archaeologically, most of Gaul in La Tène D (c. 150-30 BC) has few visible graves: cremation dominates, much of the population is archaeologically invisible (excarnation or scattering is often suggested). Elite cremation graves with Italian amphorae and bronze vessels appear late; near the Cadurci, the aristocratic grave at Boé (Agen, Nitiobriges, c. mid-1st century BC) with amphorae and a vehicle (from memory: Schönfelder 2002; verify). Exposure of war dead to vultures is attested for the Celtiberian Vaccaei (Silius Italicus III.340-343; Aelian *NA* X.22), not for Gauls. **Attested/inferred** as marked.

**Cadurci or Quercy cult evidence.** I found no verifiable pre-Roman Cadurci sanctuary. The Roman city of Divona was named for a sacred spring (*Divona* = "divine one"), with a temple succeeding the fountain cult (Rigal 2004, 2009). Hirtius VIII.43.5 says that when the Romans cut off Uxellodunum's spring, the defenders took it as the will of the gods, not the work of men. **Attested** for Uxellodunum's spring; nothing else specific.

### 2.6 Daily life and economy

**Food.** Posidonius (Athenaeus IV.36): little bread, a lot of meat, boiled, roasted over coals or on spits; they eat cleanly but like lions, lifting whole joints and tearing at them, with a small sheathed knife for the hard bits; near rivers and the sea they eat fish baked with salt, vinegar and cumin. Strabo IV.4.3: much meat, especially pork, fresh and salted; Gallic herds of swine are so large they supply Rome and Italy with salt pork and woollen cloaks. Varro *RR* II.4.10 on Gallic hams. Diodorus V.28.4: they sit on wolf- or dog-skins, served by the youngest boys and girls, with hearths full of cauldrons and spits of meat. Archaeology: cereals (barley, emmer and naked wheat, millet), pulses, pork, cattle, sheep/goat; dog and horse eaten at some sites. **Attested.**

**Bread.** Pliny XVIII.68: in Gaul and Spain they use the froth from fermenting grain-drink (beer) as leaven, "which is why their bread is lighter than others'". Pliny XVIII.62: the Gauls call a kind of grain *brace*. **Attested.** The game's "dense, sour" barley bread is not wrong for poor households, but the specifically Gallic touch is beer-barm leavened bread.

**Beer, mead and wine.** Athenaeus IV.36 (Posidonius): the rich drink wine from Italy or Massalia, unmixed or with a little water; the poorer drink a wheat beer, sometimes with honey, which they call *korma* (Gaulish *curmi*, attested on later spindle whorls). Diodorus V.26.2-3: lacking wine they make a drink from barley (*zythos*) and from water used to wash honeycombs; they are passionate about wine, drink it unmixed, and Italian traders take a slave for a jar of wine. Pliny XXII.164 records *cervesia*, a Gaulish word for beer. **Attested.**

**The wine trade in the southwest.** Italian wine came up the Aude-Garonne route through Narbo and Tolosa. Cicero (*Pro Fonteio* 19-20, c. 69 BC) describes transit dues on wine at Tolosa and at stations named Crodunum, Vulchalo and Cobiomachus (a Gaulish *-magos* name) on the road to the Ruteni. Dressel 1 amphorae occur in very large quantities in the Toulouse region and the Albigeois. At Puy d'Issolud (Uxellodunum) 336 Dressel 1 fragments, including about 100 feet, are catalogued, many Dressel 1B (Benquet). The Lucterius coin finds hint at Cadurci links north to the Seine and south to the Pyrenees (Pailler). **Attested.** I found no quantified figures for Murcens or l'Impernal, and could not verify a famous deposit in the bed of the Lot itself; treat "huge amphora deposits in the Lot" as **unverified**.

**Salt.** The "Santone salt" in the game is well chosen: the Charente estuaries hold a concentration of Iron Age salt-working sites unique in France (Perrichet-Thomas 1986; Charente gulfs research project, ADLFI). The technique was **briquetage**: brine boiled in clay troughs and moulds over fires, producing salt cakes; solar salt marshes came later, in Roman times. **Attested** (archaeology). Correction: "grey salt from the evaporation pans" should become "a cake of salt boiled down on the coast".

**Coinage.** The southwest's coinage from the 2nd century BC to the conquest was silver: the "monnaies à la croix", small drachms with a stylised head and a reverse cross with symbols in the quarters, struck by the Volcae Tectosages and their neighbours (Savès 1976; Boudet 1990). The Cadurci had their own types (dealers attribute the "type de Cuzance", c. 58-52 BC, to the Cahors region), plus Lucterius's inscribed silver and bronze. Roman denarii circulated as well. **Attested** (numismatic; dealer attributions are weaker). The game's single silver currency is fine.

**Linen: the Cadurci speciality.** Strabo IV.2.2: the Petrocorii and Bituriges Cubi have fine ironworks, the Cadurci linen workshops, the Ruteni and Gabali silver mines. Pliny XIX.8 lists the Cadurci among the Gallic linen weavers, and *cadurcum* became Latin for a linen mattress or coverlet (Juvenal 6.537). **Attested** (Strabo's date is Augustan; the industry probably predates the conquest, **inferred**). This is the best Cadurci-specific economic fact available and it is not yet in the game.

**Iron and smiths.** Iron in the southwest: Strabo IV.2.2 names the Petrocorii (Périgord, the Cadurci's northern neighbours) for ironworks. The Bituriges were skilled miners (Caesar VII.22). Smith status: Irish law later ranks the smith among privileged persons and Irish myth has a smith-god (Goibniu); in Gaul the root *gobann-* "smith" appears in the name of Vercingetorix's noble uncle Gobannitio (VII.4.2) and in the god Gobannos (Roman-period). **Inferred** (that smiths were respected in Gaul). The game's Cobnertos, valued and prosperous, is fine.

**Agriculture.** Strabo IV.1.2: Gaul produces grain, millet, acorns and every kind of livestock; olives and figs only in the south, grapes not in the north. Pliny XVII.42-47 on Gallic and British marling of fields; XVIII.296 on the Gallic reaping machine (Roman period). Storage in pits and raised granaries is standard Iron Age archaeology. **Attested/inferred.**

**Slavery.** Caesar sold the entire Aduatuci, 53,000 people (II.33.6); executed the Venetan senate and sold the rest (III.16.4); gave one captive per soldier after Alesia (VII.89.5). Plutarch (*Caesar* 15) says a million captured. Gauls held and traded slaves (Diodorus V.26.3, wine for a slave; VI.13 debt bondage; VI.19 slaves burned at funerals in the past). Drappes in the Cadurci rising of 51 BC recruited slaves by offering freedom (VIII.30.1). **Attested.** Archaeological iron gang-chains and shackles are known from late Iron Age Gaul and Britain (Thompson 2003, *The Archaeology of Greek and Roman Slavery*).

**Calendar and time.** Caesar VI.18.2: time counted by nights, birthdays and month- and year-beginnings start with night. Tacitus says the same of Germans (*Germania* 11). Pliny XVI.250: druids begin months and years and a 30-year cycle on the moon's sixth day. The Coligny calendar (bronze, Ain, late 2nd century AD) is a five-year lunisolar cycle of 62 months, each split into halves by *atenoux* ("returning night"), with month names Samonios, Dumannios, Riuros, Anagantios, Ogronnios, Cutios, Giamonios, Simiuisonnos, Equos, Elembiuos, Edrinios, Cantlos, and day marks MAT (good) and ANM (not good). **Attested** for the 2nd century AD; using its month names in 52 BC is an **inference** (the system is probably old, and its structure fits Pliny), so fine for flavor, not for exact dates. Which month falls when is **disputed** (Samonios as early summer or early winter).

Other daily-life details: sons may not approach their father in public until old enough to bear arms (VI.18.3); hair lime-washed, nobles shave but keep long moustaches that strain their drink (Diodorus V.28.2-3); gold torques and bracelets (Strabo IV.4.5); Gauls and Britons wore rings on the middle finger (Pliny XXXIII.24); Gauls invented soap for reddening hair (Pliny XXVIII.191).

### 2.7 Language and names

**What Gaulish is attested.** Gaulish is a Celtic language known from about 800-1000 inscriptions (RIG, *Recueil des inscriptions gauloises*), coin legends, names in Greek and Latin authors, place names and loanwords. Main texts:
- **Gallo-Greek** inscriptions (3rd-1st century BC), mainly in the lower Rhône and Narbonnaise, written in Greek letters (Caesar VI.14.4; the Helvetian census tablets in Greek letters, I.29.1). Typical dedication formula: *Segomaros Ouilloneos tooutious Namausatis eiōrou Bēlēsami sosin nemēton*, "Segomaros son of Villu, citizen of Nemausus, offered this sanctuary to Belesama" (Vaison, RIG G-153).
- **Gallo-Latin** inscriptions (1st century BC onward): Alise-Sainte-Reine, *Martialis Dannotali ieuru Ucuete sosin celicnon* ("Martialis son of Dannotalos offered this building to Ucuetis", RIG L-13); Genouilly, *ratin briuatiom* ("the rampart of the bridge-people"?).
- **Chamalières** lead tablet (c. AD 50, Auvergne): an invocation of Maponos with a list of named men; contains a form often read as "who will swear" (*toncsiíontío*). Translations differ widely (Lambert, Fleuriot, Koch).
- **Larzac** lead tablet (c. AD 100, Ruteni country): the longest text, a magical dispute among women; *bnanom brictom* "the magic of the women", *duxtir* "daughter", *matir* "mother".
- **La Graufesenque** (Millau, Ruteni, 1st century AD): potters' kiln tallies with Gaulish ordinals: *cintux* (first), *allos* (second), *tritios*, *petuar(ios)*, *pinpetos*, *suexos*, *sextametos*, *oxtumetos*, *nametos*, *decametos*.
- **Châteaubleau** tile (3rd-4th century), a love or marriage text; **Coligny** calendar (2nd century); spindle whorls from central Gaul (3rd century) with lines such as *nata uimpi curmi da* ("pretty girl, give beer").
- **Coin legends** of the 50s BC in Latin letters: VERCINGETORIXS (Arverni), DVBNOREIX (Dumnorix, Aedui), LVXTIIRIOS (Lucterius, Cadurci), ADIETVANVS (Sotiates), EPAD (Epasnactus), CISIAMBOS CATTOS VERCOBRETO (Lexovii).
- **Glossed words**: Caesar's *ambactus*, *vergobretus*, *soldurii*; Pliny's *cervesia*, *brace*; Venantius Fortunatus (*Carm.* I.9) explains *Vernemetis* as "great sanctuary in the Gaulish tongue"; Endlicher's Glossary (5th-6th century) gives *dunum* "hill", *nanto* "valley", *onno* "river", *briua* "bridge", *condate* "confluence", *ambe* "stream", *are-* "before" (Aremorici "by the sea").

**Name formation (attested patterns).** Masculine nominative *-os* (Latinised *-us*), feminine *-a*; consonant-stem *-rix* "king" (Dumnorix, Vercingetorix, Orgetorix). Compounds of two elements are the noble norm: *Catu-* "battle", *Dumno-/Dubno-* "world/deep", *Ollo-* "great/all", *Sego-* "strength/victory", *Uindo-* "white/fair", *Bitu-* "world/life", *Teuto-/Touto-* "people", *Epo-* "horse", *Taruo-* "bull", *Matu-* "good" (or "bear"), *Cintu-* "first", *Seno-* "old", *Lugu-* (the god Lugus), *-maros* "great" (Segomaros, Viridomaros), *-genos/-gnatos* "born" (Cintugnatos, Matugenos), *-uiros* "man", *-ualos* "ruler" (Catuvalos), *-cnos/-icnos* patronymic or "son of" (Larzac *Severa Tertionicna*; Toutissicnos), diminutives *-illos/-illa*, adjectival *-acos/-icos*. Patronymics: "X son of Y" is written with Y's name in the genitive (*Martialis Dannotali*) or with *-icnos* (*Iccios Cobnerticnos* would be "Iccios son of Cobnertos").

**Place-name elements.** *-dunon* fort/hill (Uxellodunum "high fort", Noviodunum "new fort", Lugdunum, Vellaunodunum), *-magos* plain, then market (Rotomagus, Noviomagus, Cobiomachus), *-duron* enclosure/market (Octodurus), *-ialon* clearing (Nantoialon > Nanteuil), *-ritu-* ford (Augustoritum, Ritumagus, Darioritum), *nemeton* sanctuary (Nemetodurum, Vernemetum), *condate* confluence, *-briua* bridge (Samarobriva), *-ona/-onna* for rivers and springs (Divona, Matrona, Axona). *-briga* "hill" is mostly Iberian-peninsula and rare in Gaul. *-acon/-acum* names are overwhelmingly Roman-period estate names (fundus of So-and-so); avoid them for 52 BC.

**The Cadurci's own names.** *Cadurci* may be *Catu-turcoi*, "battle-boars" (Pailler 2012), **uncertain**. Their 52 BC chief town is unknown: Murcens and l'Impernal (Luzech) are big oppida with murus gallicus defences; Uxellodunum is Puy d'Issolud (Vayrac/Saint-Denis-lès-Martel), identified by the Fontaine de Loulié excavations (Girault 2013, Bibracte 23), accepted by the Ministry of Culture in 2001. **Divona** (Cahors) is Ptolemy's *Douēona* (II.7.9 or II.7.11 depending on edition), but it was founded under Augustus or Tiberius on a new site; nothing places a pre-Roman town there (Rigal 2009). Note that Ausonius's "Divona, in the Celtic tongue a spring added to the gods" (*Ordo urbium nobilium* XX) refers to a spring at Bordeaux, not Cahors.

**Attested Cadurci and southwestern names** for designers to use or echo:
Cadurci: Lucterius / LVXTIIRIOS (Caesar VII.5; coins); Lucterius Senecianus and his son Marcus Lucterius Leo (CIL XIII 1541; Roman-period). Nitiobriges: Teutomatus, son of Ollovico (VII.31.5). Sotiates: Adiatuanus (III.22; coins ADIETVANVS). Arverni: Vercingetorix, Celtillus, Gobannitio, Vercassivellaunus, Epasnactus, Critognatus. Senones (allied in the Cadurci war): Drappes. Lemovices: Sedullus (VII.88). Santones coins: ARIVOS, CONTOVTOS, ATECTORI (dealer readings). Ruteni country (Larzac, Graufesenque): Severa Tertionicna and potters' names such as Cintux-derived ordinals. Aquitanian (non-Celtic, southwest of the Garonne; Caesar I.1 says their language differs): Roman-period names like Andere, Nescato, Cison, Belex, useful for a traveller from beyond the Garonne.

**Naming rules for inventing more.**
1. Nobles get two-element compounds (Catu-maros, Sego-uindos, Ollo-genos); commoners can get one element plus a suffix (Moccos, Banna, Cottos, Dubnacos, Uindilla).
2. Masculine *-os* in Gaulish-speaking mouths, *-us* in Roman documents; feminine *-a*; *-rix* only for men of rank, and sparingly (it means "king").
3. Use patronymics in introductions: "Iccios son of Cobnertos" or *Cobnerticnos*.
4. Avoid naming people straight after gods (Epona, Taranis, Lugus) without a suffix; theophoric names add an element (Lugu-selua, Epo-redo-rix, Eponina).
5. Settlements: describe the site in two elements, the second from the list above. Sound pattern: no *f* at word start, no *p* in native words except loans (Gaulish lost Indo-European *p*), *x* for the *ch* sound (LVXTIIRIOS), *ð/θ* "tau gallicum" for a *ts/th*-like sound.
6. Don't invent "Gaulish phrases" for real dialogue; Gaulish syntax is barely known. Single words and names carry the flavor safely.

**References for the language.** Delamarre, *Dictionnaire de la langue gauloise* (Errance, 2nd ed. 2003): the standard word list with etymologies. Lambert, *La langue gauloise* (Errance, rev. 2003): grammar and the main texts with commentary. Also: Evans, *Gaulish Personal Names* (1967); Delamarre, *Noms de personnes celtiques dans l'épigraphie classique* (2007); RIG (Gallia supplement 45, 1985-2002); Matasović, *Etymological Dictionary of Proto-Celtic* (2009); Mullen and Ruiz Darasse, *Gaulish* open-access guide (LatinNow, 2018).

### 2.8 Oaths, greetings, insults and flavor for dialogue

- **Oath on the standards.** The Carnutes, unable to exchange hostages without the plot leaking, ask the others to bind themselves by oath "with the military standards gathered together, which by their custom is the most solemn ceremony" (VII.2.2). **Attested.** Already in the intro text.
- **The cavalry oath.** Before the cavalry battle of 52 BC Vercingetorix's horsemen swear the most sacred oath that no man shall be received under a roof, nor approach his children, parents or wife, unless he has ridden twice through the enemy column (VII.66.7). **Attested.** A ready-made heroic vow.
- **Oath of the soldurii.** Share everything in life, die with the lord (III.22). **Attested** (Sotiates).
- **The sky falling.** Celts on the Danube told Alexander they feared nothing except that the sky might fall on them (Strabo VII.3.8, from Ptolemy I). Irish oaths a thousand years later run "unless the sky falls on us, the earth opens under us, the sea drowns us". Use the first as attested Celtic bravado; the Irish formula only as clearly labeled inspiration.
- **News at the gate.** Gauls make travellers stop and tell what they've heard (IV.5.2). **Attested.** Make every NPC demand news.
- **Debt beyond death.** Loans to be repaid in the next world (Valerius Maximus II.6.10; Mela III.19). **Attested.** "Pay me in the next world, then."
- **Boasting and challenge.** Diodorus V.29.2-3: before battle they step out, challenge the best of the enemy to single combat, sing of their ancestors' deeds and their own, and belittle the opponent. V.31.1: they speak in riddles, hint, say much by implication, exaggerate to praise themselves and run others down, and are boastful and threatening. **Attested.**
- **Mockery of Romans.** The Aduatuci jeered from their wall at "men so small" building such a big siege tower (Caesar II.30.3-4: Gauls despise Roman shortness). A Gaul stuck his tongue out at Manlius Torquatus before single combat (Livy VII.10.5, Claudius Quadrigarius in Gellius IX.13). **Attested.**
- **Bards' praise and blame.** Diodorus V.31.2; Posidonius's poet and Louernios (Athenaeus IV.37); companions who sing their lord's praises in war (Athenaeus VI.49). Irish satire that raises blisters is much later, label it if used.
- **Heads.** "Not for its weight in gold" (Diodorus V.29.5, Strabo IV.4.5).
- **"Vae victis".** Brennus's words in Livy V.48.9 (390 BC); the game title's source. Roman-reported.
- **Toasts and drink.** Drinking from a shared cup, a little at a time but often (Athenaeus IV.36); *curmi da*, "give beer" (spindle whorl, 3rd century AD).
- **Greetings.** None attested in Gaulish. Invent; do not present invented phrases as real Gaulish.
- **Curses and swearing by gods.** No Gaulish "by Taranis!"-style expletive is attested. Defixiones (Larzac, Chamalières) show cursing by writing names on lead and invoking underworld powers; *brictom* "magic/spell" is a usable word.

## 3. Check against the game

Each item: what the game has, what the evidence says, what to do. Ordered by importance.

1. **Grotto lore: Taranis's share of the dead hung in high places** (`brig_vates.dlg` `lore`, `grotto_dubnacos.dlg` `lore`). The only source that ties a killing method to a god (Berne scholia on Lucan I.445) gives *hanging on trees* to **Esus**, burning in a wooden vessel to Taranis. **Correction:** make the hanged dead "Esus's share", or an unnamed old god. "Hung for the birds" (world.json) echoes exposure of war dead, attested for the Celtiberian Vaccaei, not Gauls; fine as local invention.

2. **The shrine robbery's moral weight** (`brig_vates`, `brig_vellaunos` `pardon`). Caesar VI.17.5 (death with torture for taking from sacred deposits), VI.16.5 (thieves preferred as sacrifices), Diodorus V.27.4 (sacred gold untouched from fear). Vellaunos treats it as an aggravation of a cow-killing. **Correction:** someone (Cintugnatos, Senovir, or a visiting druid) should say Dubnacos has now earned death, not just exile; the pardon route should cost more or involve the vates, and returning the shrine goods (idol, silver) should matter. This also strengthens the Lore-scare route, which already leans on dread of the gods.

3. **Who judged Dubnacos** (`brig_vellaunos`: "Seven winters' exile, that was the judgment"). Caesar VI.13.5-6 gives killings and their penalties to the druids. **Correction:** say the judgment came from a druid (perhaps at the Carnute assembly, or a regional one), with the elders enforcing it; or let Vellaunos commute it only with the vates' agreement. Blood-price in cattle is **inferred** (Irish *éraic*, Germanic wergild); no Gallic text mentions it, but nothing contradicts it. Exile for killing a fellow citizen is directly supported by Nicolaus F5. Keep.

4. **The altar stone and offering box** (`brig_vates` start). Late La Tène sanctuaries are ditched enclosures with pits ("hollow altars"), posts, and deposits in ditches and pits; Roman-style stone altars come after the conquest. **Correction:** "altar stone" becomes "the offering pit" or "the sacred enclosure's post"; the "offering box" can stay (a wooden chest is unprovable but harmless), or become a pit or heap the robbers dug into, which is closer to Caesar VI.17.4.

5. **Epona, the player's sister** (`story.json`, intro). Epona is a goddess's name, attested mostly in the Roman period. **Suggestion:** use *Eponina* (attested personal name, Tacitus *Hist.* IV.67) or another Epo- compound (Epomara, Eposenia, invented but well-formed). If T wants to keep Epona, it's not impossible, just unattested.

6. **The hostages' social rank** (`brig_vellaunos`, `brig_cobnertos`). Caesar's hostages are nobles' children, given by a civitas or a leader. Ateula (chief elder's daughter) and the player's sister fit if the families are of the fort's leading kin. **Iccios, the smith's son, is the odd one.** Either make Cobnertos a man of standing (a smith-client of the patron noble, plausibly respected; see 2.6) or explain why a commoner's boy was taken (substituted for a noble's child: the villager and Dubnacos lines about "nearly being one of the three" already point this way, which is good). Also note that historically hostages were demanded from the Cadurci as a people; a single village giving three is unusual. A line that "the Cadurci gave hostages, and Brigodunon's share was three" fixes it.

7. **Did the Cadurci give Rome hostages five years ago (57 BC)?** No text says the Cadurci submitted before 52 BC; Crassus campaigned among the Aquitani in 56 BC (III.20-27) and the maritime peoples in 57 (II.34). The intro's "when Rome's reach came as far as the Cadurci" is **plausible but unattested**; 56 BC (Crassus's campaign next door) is the more natural date. Not contradicted.

8. **Divona/Cahors.** If anything in the full game names "Divona" or Cahors as a Cadurci town in 52 BC, cut it: Divona is Augustan or later. Uxellodunum on its cliff above the Duranius (world.json) is correct.

9. **Salt** (`items.json` salt: "Grey salt from the evaporation pans"). Iron Age Atlantic salt was boiled (briquetage) and sold as cakes. **Correction:** "A cake of coarse salt, boiled down from seawater on the Santone coast." The salt trader's "Santone salt" Lore check is good.

10. **Bread** (`items.json`: "Dense, sour, filling"). Pliny XVIII.68: Gallic bread was leavened with beer froth and lighter than others'. **Optional tweak:** "Barley bread raised with beer barm. Lighter than it looks." Also Posidonius says Gauls ate little bread and much meat.

11. **Wine** (`items.json` roman_wine). "A jar has bought a slave" (Diodorus V.26.3) and "drink it undiluted" (Diodorus V.26.3; Athenaeus IV.36) are both attested. **Confirmed.** The salt trader's "Wine I sell to people who can pay" is apt. Note that Dressel 1 amphorae reached Uxellodunum (Benquet).

12. **Linen** (not in game). The Cadurci were *the* linen people (Strabo IV.2.2; Pliny XIX.8; *cadurcum*). **Addition:** Padded linen coat description could say "Cadurci linen, the best in Gaul"; Brigodunon's women weaving linen, linen as a trade good on the salt road.

13. **The vates** (Cintugnatos). Seer, sacrificer, healer, shrine-keeper fits Strabo IV.4.4 and Diodorus V.31. **Confirmed.** Diodorus's "no sacrifice without a philosopher present" suggests a druid outranks him and should be mentioned as existing somewhere.

14. **Elders and chief elder** (Vellaunos). Elders acting for a community: Caesar II.13, II.28. **Confirmed.** Add, if wanted, a patron noble above Brigodunon (the Hirtius VIII.32 clientela model), which also explains why Lucterius's riders matter so much (`calendar.json` day 12: good, attested).

15. **Rumour control and news** (`brig_villager`, `vind_trader`, `moccos`). Caesar VI.20 (news must go to the magistrate, not the street) and IV.5 (travellers stopped for news) are both usable. The villager's "Don't tell anyone I said so" is right on.

16. **Sulicena as headwoman.** Unattested for Gaul, not contradicted; see 2.4. **Keep**, maybe have someone remark on it.

17. **Gods' share / grandmother's lore / "the thunderer"** (`brig_vates` lore). Taranis as thunderer is fine (the name is from *taran-* "thunder"). See item 1 for what's wrong.

18. **"Seven winters", "three winters"**. Gauls counted by nights (VI.18.2). Winters are fine for years; for short spans, have characters count nights ("in three nights", "ten nights' ride"). Haldo's Germans counted nights too (Tacitus *Germania* 11).

19. **Elder's iron ring** (`items.json`). No evidence either way for an iron ring as a token of authority; Pliny XXXIII.24 says Gauls wore rings on the middle finger. Harmless.

20. **Grain writ, a split hazel tally** (`items.json`). Unattested, plausible. Gauls kept accounts in Greek letters (VI.14.4), so a written tablet would also be plausible for a trader like Sulicena.

21. **The hostage tally with Roman letters** (`items.json`). No evidence that Romans gave receipts for hostages. It's a game device; fine as long as nobody claims it was standard.

22. **Writing Gaulish in Greek letters** (DESIGN.md Skills). Caesar VI.14 says Greek letters were used for accounts, not druidic doctrine; Gallo-Greek inscriptions cluster in the Rhône valley and Narbonnaise. In the southwest in the 50s BC, Lucterius's own coins are in **Latin** letters. **Correction:** the Lore skill should cover reading Gaulish in Greek *and* Latin letters; Latin letters were already the norm on southwestern coins by 52 BC.

23. **The Grotto idol** (wooden, carved from an oak root). Lucan III.412-413 describes crude, artless images of gods cut from tree trunks in the Massilia grove; wooden figures survive from Gallic and Roman-period sites (Chamalières and Sources-de-la-Seine ex-votos are Roman-period). **Confirmed** as plausible.

24. **Torques** (`items.json` gold_torque: "the kind of thing a man is buried in"). Late La Tène gold torques in the southwest mostly turn up in hoards and sacred deposits rather than graves (to verify). **Suggestion:** "the kind of thing a man gives to the gods, or is buried with if he's very great".

25. **Haldo and his men** (`ford_haldo.dlg`, npcs.json). Caesar had about 400 German horse from the start (VII.13.1) and sent across the Rhine for more in 52 BC (VII.65.4); the Ubii were allied (IV.16). Fair hair knotted on the side is the Suebian knot (Tacitus *Germania* 38), so Haldo reads as a Sueb, not a Batavian or Ubian. Caesar's cavalry in Britain was Gallic (V.5), so "rode in Britannia" is unsupported but not impossible. Desertion in autumn 53 is invention, not contradicted. Name: see section below.

| **Vellaunos** | Well-formed | God Vellaunus (Mercury Vellaunus at Hières-sur-Amby; Ocelus Vellaunus, RIB 309); in Cassivellaunus, Vercassivellaunus; town Vellaunodunum (VII.11). Meaning disputed ("good" or "ruler"). | Keep. |
| **Dubnacos** | Well-formed | *Dubno-/Dumno-* both attested (DVBNOREIX on Dumnorix's coins); Dumnacus, leader of the Andes (VIII.26). | Keep. |
| **Lugurix** | Plausible, attestation unverified | *Lugu-* + *-rix*; one secondary source cites Lugurix as an attested name, unconfirmed. Lugotorix (V.22) is British and may mean "mouse-king". | Keep, or use *Lugurix* only if he's a man of rank (*-rix*); for a merchant, *Luguselos* or *Lugaucos* are humbler. |
| **Sulicena** | Attested once, uncertain reading | RIB 134 (Lasborough, Britain), reading possibly SVIICENA. *Suli-* is common (Sulis). | Keep. |
| **Cintugnatos** | Attested | Cintugnatus on several Roman-period inscriptions (Mainz, Wittelsheim, Metz; EDH). "First-born". | Keep. |
| **Cobnertos** | Attested | Potter Cobnertus at Lezoux, 1st c. AD. | Keep. |
| **Moccos** | Attested | Moccus, epithet of Mercury (Langres) and personal name; *moccos* "pig" (from memory; Delamarre). | Keep. |
| **Iccios** | Attested | Iccius of the Remi (Caesar II.3, II.6). | Keep. |
| **Ateula** | Attested as coin legend | ATEVLA / VLATOS silver, attributed by dealers to the Remi area, c. 60-30 BC. | Keep. |
| **Epona** | Divine name | See item 5. | *Eponina*. |
| **Senovir** | Well-formed | *Seno-* "old" + *-uiros* "man". | *Senouiros* in Gaulish form, Senovir as a clipped form is fine. |
| **Cottos** | Attested | Cottius, king of the Alpine Cottii. | Keep. |
| **Banna** | Unverified as a person's name | *banna* "peak/horn" is a word and a Roman fort name. | Keep, or *Bannia*. |
| **Dannotalos** | Attested | *Martialis Dannotali* at Alise-Sainte-Reine (RIG L-13). | Keep. |
| **Haldo** | Germanic, plausible | A weak (*-o*) masculine of the later Old High German type; first-century Roman sources Latinise Germanic names (Ariovistus, Nasua, Cimberius, Chariovalda, Boiocalus, Malorix, Verritus). | Keep; Gauls and Romans might call him *Haldus*. |
